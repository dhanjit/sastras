Pramāṇa-śāstra, the theory of knowledge, was a field of Sanskrit writing for close to two thousand years:cite[sep]. Every major school took part, and each argued about knowledge from within its own account of what is real:cite[sep].

Its key word, pramāṇa, is a means of knowing: whatever produces pramā, true knowledge:cite[wiki]. It forms a triad with the knower (pramātṛ) and the knowable (prameya):cite[wiki]. As the texts use the word, a pramāṇa never yields a false cognition:cite[sep].:note[Success is built into the word. Seeming to see a snake that is not there is not a faulty perception but a “pseudo-perception” (pratyakṣābhāsa): one merely seems to see:cite[sep].]

Many texts list six pramāṇas:cite[wiki]. Three are the principal ones: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna) and testimony (śabda):cite[sep]. Three more are disputed: comparison (upamāna), postulation (arthāpatti) and non-perception (anupalabdhi), the knowing of an absence:cite[wiki].

The chips on the [map](/) show how many each school accepts. For Cārvāka only perception counts:cite[sep]; some accounts add that it used inference while denying it could ever be certain:cite[wiki-carvaka]. Buddhists accept perception and inference:cite[wiki]. Jains, Sāṅkhya and Yoga add testimony:cite[wiki]. Nyāya adds comparison:cite[wiki]. Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā adds postulation, and Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā and [Advaita Vedānta](/advaita-vedanta) accept all six:cite[wiki]. Where branches of a school differ, the map names the one it follows: Nyāya, not Vaiśeṣika, which keeps only perception and inference; Bhāṭṭa, not Prābhākara; Advaita, not Viśiṣṭādvaita or Dvaita, which accept three:cite[wiki].

The dashed square is inference, which some accounts say Cārvāka used while denying it could ever be certain:cite[wiki-carvaka].]{#pramana-sastra-ladder}

The schools also argue over whether one source reduces to another:cite[wiki]. Nyāya treats postulation as a kind of inference, while Vaiśeṣika and Yogācāra Buddhists treat testimony as an inference from the speaker's reliability:cite[sep].

Each tradition wrote its own treatises:cite[sep]. Nyāya works from Gautama's Nyāyasūtra and its commentaries:cite[sep]. Kumārila, whose followers form the Bhāṭṭa branch of Mīmāṃsā:cite[wiki], treats perception in his Ślokavārttika:cite[sep]. For Buddhists the key texts are Dignāga's Pramāṇasamuccaya and Dharmakīrti's Pramāṇavārttika:cite[wiki]. Nyāya's later classic, the Tattvacintāmaṇi of [Navya-Nyāya](/navya-nyaya), gives one part to each of the school's four pramāṇas:cite[sep-navya].
