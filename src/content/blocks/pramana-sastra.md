Pramāṇa-śāstra is the theory of knowledge, a genre of Sanskrit writing that spans almost twenty centuries:cite[sep]. It was carried on inside the schools: no author treats knowledge apart from their school's view of what exists:cite[sep].

Its key word, pramāṇa, is a means of knowing: whatever produces pramā, true knowledge:cite[wiki]. It forms a triad with the knower (pramātṛ) and the knowable (prameya):cite[wiki]. As the texts use the word, a pramāṇa never yields a false cognition:cite[sep].:note[Success is built into the word. Seeming to see a snake that is not there is not a faulty perception but a “pseudo-perception” (pratyakṣābhāsa): one merely seems to see:cite[sep].]

Many texts list six pramāṇas:cite[wiki]. Three are almost universally accepted: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna) and testimony (śabda):cite[wiki]. Three are disputed: comparison (upamāna), postulation (arthāpatti) and non-perception (anupalabdhi), the knowing of an absence:cite[wiki].

How many a school accepts is what the chips on the [map](/) show. For Cārvāka only perception counts:cite[sep]. Buddhists accept perception and inference:cite[wiki]. Jains, Sāṅkhya and Yoga add testimony:cite[wiki]. Nyāya adds comparison:cite[wiki]. Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā adds postulation, and Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā and [Advaita Vedānta](/advaita-vedanta) accept all six:cite[wiki]. Where branches of a school differ, the map names the one it follows: Nyāya, not Vaiśeṣika, which keeps only perception and inference; Bhāṭṭa, not Prābhākara; Advaita, not Viśiṣṭādvaita or Dvaita, which accept three:cite[wiki].

The schools also argue over whether one source reduces to another:cite[wiki]. Nyāya treats postulation as a kind of inference, while Vaiśeṣika and Yogācāra Buddhists treat testimony as an inference from the speaker's reliability:cite[sep].

Each tradition built its own treatises. Nyāya works from Gautama's Nyāyasūtra and its commentaries:cite[sep]. Kumārila, whose followers form the Bhāṭṭa branch of Mīmāṃsā:cite[wiki], treats perception in his Ślokavārttika:cite[sep]. For Buddhists the key texts are Dignāga's Pramāṇasamuccaya and Dharmakīrti's Pramāṇavārttika:cite[wiki]. Nyāya's later classic, the Tattvacintāmaṇi of [Navya-Nyāya](/navya-nyaya), gives one part to each of the school's four pramāṇas:cite[sep-navya].
