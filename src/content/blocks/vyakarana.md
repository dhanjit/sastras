Vyākaraṇa, literally “analysis”, is the study of Sanskrit grammar:cite[wiki]. It is one of the six Vedāṅgas, the auxiliary disciplines of Vedic study, beside phonetics (śikṣā), metre (chandas), etymology (nirukta), ritual (kalpa) and astronomy (jyotiṣa):cite[wiki-vedanga]. These arose as the language of the Veda grew archaic:cite[wiki-vedanga]; grammar's task was to fix the exact form of words and guard the texts against careless use and transmission:cite[wiki].

Its three most famous grammarians each built on the one before:cite[wiki-mahabhasya]. Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī (“Eight Chapters”) sets out the language in about 4,000 sūtras:cite[brit]. Its date is disputed: Britannica places it in the 6th–5th century BCE:cite[brit], while several specialists, pointing to coins named in its rules, argue for the mid-4th century BCE:cite[wiki-panini]. Kātyāyana (c. 3rd century BCE) added vārttikas, notes that analyse, refine and in places emend Pāṇini's rules:cite[wiki]. Patañjali's Mahābhāṣya (“Great Commentary”, c. 2nd century BCE) discusses about a third of Pāṇini's rules together with Kātyāyana's notes:cite[wiki-mahabhasya]; written as a dialogue, it has been called “the classical model for academic texts”:cite[wiki].

The Aṣṭādhyāyī does not only describe Sanskrit; its rules can generate it:cite[brit]. It takes lists of verb roots and nominal stems as input and gives rules that derive well-formed words from them, with metarules for settling conflicts between rules:cite[wiki-panini]. Its rules are concise formulas:cite[wiki-panini].:note[Take rule 1.1.2, adeṅ guṇaḥ. In two words it gives the vowels a, e and o the technical name guṇa; eṅ is a code standing for e and o:cite[wiki-kaumudi]. Such codes are drawn from the lists of sounds that open the grammar:cite[wiki].] Known in Europe from the nineteenth century, it influenced modern linguists such as Leonard Bloomfield, though how far it shaped Ferdinand de Saussure is debated:cite[wiki-panini].

::figure[A schematic, not a worked derivation. Read it from the top: lists of verb roots and nominal stems go in, rules (sūtras) derive a well-formed word from them, and where two rules conflict, a metarule settles which one applies:cite[wiki-panini].]{#vyakarana-derivation}

Pāṇini's own order is hard to learn from, since one derivation draws on rules from different chapters:cite[wiki-kaumudi]. In the 17th century Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita, working in Varanasi, regrouped the sūtras by topic in his Siddhāntakaumudī:cite[wiki-bhattoji], basing it on an earlier work, the Prakriyākaumudī:cite[wiki-kaumudi]. His pupil Varadarāja wrote a shorter version, the Laghu-siddhāntakaumudī:cite[wiki-kaumudi]. Bhaṭṭoji's works are still studied today:cite[wiki-bhattoji].
