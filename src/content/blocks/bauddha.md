Buddhist philosophy begins with the Buddha's account of suffering (duḥkha, Pāli dukkha): its causes and its end:cite[sep]:cite[brit-india].:note[When he lived is uncertain: older scholarship put him at about 560–480 BCE, while many scholars now date his death to around 405 BCE:cite[sep].] As the tradition reads him, the ignorance behind suffering is the conceit of an “I” and a “mine”:cite[sep]. This is the teaching of not-self (anattā, Sanskrit anātman):cite[brit-india], which the [Ātman](/atman) page compares with other schools' views of the self.

Its other face is dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda, Pāli paṭiccasamuppāda):cite[iep]:cite[wiki], common to every school: what arises does so on conditions, and stops when they stop:cite[wiki]. Explaining a life as a causal series with no owner gives the Buddha a middle path between an eternal self and annihilation at death:cite[sep]. The best-known form has twelve links (nidāna): ignorance (avidyā), volitional formations (saṃskāra), consciousness (vijñāna), name-and-form (nāmarūpa), the six sense bases (ṣaḍāyatana), contact (sparśa), feeling (vedanā), craving (tṛṣṇā), clinging (upādāna), becoming (bhava), birth (jāti), and ageing-and-death (jarāmaraṇa):cite[wiki].:note[The Pāli names, in the same order: avijjā, saṅkhāra, viññāṇa, nāmarūpa, saḷāyatana, phassa, vedanā, taṇhā, upādāna, bhava, jāti, jarāmaraṇa:cite[wiki]. Theravāda and Sarvāstivāda commentators spread the twelve over three lives; another reading, also Theravāda, finds in them the rise of the notion of “I” and “mine”:cite[wiki]. Mark Siderits notes that modern scholars see the twelvefold list as a later compilation: the discourses give it in two versions that differ a little, and also have shorter chains with only some of its links:cite[sep].]

Where is the cycle cut? The Four Noble Truths trace suffering to craving, and teach that craving can be ended:cite[brit]. In the discourses the chain is also undone from its root: once ignorance fades entirely, each link after it ceases in turn:cite[wiki]. Jitendra Mohanty joins the two: insight destroys ignorance, and craving and suffering go with it:cite[brit-india].:note[Craving needs more than feeling; ignorance is also among its conditions. Peter Harvey points out that a buddha still feels but does not crave, and Bhikkhu Bodhi that a chain in which each factor necessitated the next “could never be broken”:cite[wiki].]

The discourses call all conditioned things impermanent (anitya, Pāli anicca):cite[sep-abhidharma], though not yet momentary:cite[sep]. Abhidharma (Pāli Abhidhamma), the analysis of experience into elementary events (dharmas), took that step:cite[sep-abhidharma]: the Sarvāstivāda for everything, later Theravāda commentators for the mental alone:cite[sep-abhidharma]. A seemingly lasting thing is a stream (santāna) of such events, each vanishing as it arises and passing on to the next too fast to notice:cite[sep-abhidharma].:note[Dharmakīrti later argued for it in general: to be real is to have causal power, and nothing unchanging could produce effects, either all at once or one after another, so whatever exists lasts a moment:cite[sep-dharmakirti].]

The Sarvāstivādins (“all exists”) held that dharmas exist in past, present and future; the Sautrāntikas (“those who rely on the sūtras”) admitted only present ones:cite[sep-abhidharma]. Both main Mahāyāna schools of India took their questions from Abhidharma:cite[sep-abhidharma]:cite[sep-yogacara]. Madhyamaka, the “middle way” school built on Nāgārjuna (c. 150 CE), finds nothing ultimately real: whatever arises dependently lacks a nature of its own (svabhāva), and so is empty (śūnya):cite[iep]. Yogācāra, systematised by Asaṅga and Vasubandhu (4th–5th century), teaches “cognition only” (vijñaptimātra) and a store consciousness (ālayavijñāna):cite[sep-yogacara].:note[Whether this is idealism is disputed. The traditionally dominant reading, held by Lambert Schmithausen and Mark Siderits among others, says Yogācāra denies anything beyond the mind; Thomas Kochumuttom, Richard Hayes and others take it to limit only what cognition can reach, and Dan Lusthaus reads it as phenomenology:cite[sep-yogacara]. Even as idealism it is a Buddhist kind: the mind it puts first is no self, and no mental substance either, individual or universal:cite[sep-yogacara].]

The pramāṇa tradition of Dignāga (c. 480–540) and Dharmakīrti (6th or 7th century, disputed) led the Buddhist side in debate with non-Buddhist schools:cite[sep-dharmakirti].:note[It had no Sanskrit name; Tibetans called its followers “those who follow reasoning”, and Tom Tillemans calls the modern label, Pramāṇavāda (“doctrine of valid knowledge”), a “convenient misnomer”:cite[sep-dharmakirti]:cite[sep-yogacara].] The map's two chips, perception (pratyakṣa) and inference (anumāna), are its count:cite[wiki-bphil]: Dignāga admits two because objects are of two kinds, particulars and universals:cite[sep-dharmakirti].:note[Scripture, for Dharmakīrti, is at best a fallible kind of inference, to be relied on only where perception and reasoning cannot reach, such as the details of karma:cite[sep-dharmakirti].] [Pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) compares the other schools' counts.
