Buddhist philosophy begins with the Buddha's account of suffering (duḥkha, Pāli dukkha): its causes and its end:cite[sep]:cite[brit-india].:note[Many scholars now put his death near 405 BCE, later than the once-standard 560–480 BCE:cite[sep].] For the Buddha, suffering rests on ignorance of what we are, chiefly on taking things as “I” and “mine”:cite[sep]. The teaching of not-self (anattā, Sanskrit anātman) finds nothing in a person but five changing aggregates:cite[brit-india]:cite[sep]; [Ātman](/atman) compares it with other views of the self.:note[How far the denial reaches is disputed. Indian philosophers of every school, Buddhist or not, took the Buddha to reject any self; Miri Albahari and others hold that he denied only that the aggregates are a self, and some treat not-self as practical advice rather than metaphysics:cite[sep]. Jitendra Mohanty reads it as phenomenology, a description rather than a theory:cite[brit-india].]

Its other face is dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda, Pāli paṭiccasamuppāda):cite[iep]:cite[wiki], common to every school: things arise on conditions and stop when they stop:cite[wiki]. Treating a life as an ownerless causal series gives the Buddha a middle path between an eternal self and annihilation at death:cite[sep]. The best-known form has twelve links (nidāna): ignorance (avidyā), volitional formations (saṃskāra), consciousness (vijñāna), name-and-form (nāmarūpa), the six sense bases (ṣaḍāyatana), contact (sparśa), feeling (vedanā), craving (tṛṣṇā), clinging (upādāna), becoming (bhava), birth (jāti), and ageing-and-death (jarāmaraṇa):cite[wiki].:note[The Pāli names, in the same order: avijjā, saṅkhāra, viññāṇa, nāmarūpa, saḷāyatana, phassa, vedanā, taṇhā, upādāna, bhava, jāti, jarāmaraṇa:cite[wiki]. Theravāda and Sarvāstivāda commentators spread the twelve over three lives, while the Abhidharma of both schools (the Vibhaṅga, the Vijñānakāya, the Abhidharmakośa) also runs them within a single moment of mind:cite[wiki]. Among modern readers, Eviatar Shulman and Sue Hamilton take the chain as an account of mental processes, and Bhikkhu Buddhadāsa takes its birth and death to be the arising and passing of the ego:cite[wiki]. Its age is disputed too. Mark Siderits counts the twelvefold list a later compilation, pointing to the discourses' variant and shorter chains:cite[sep]; Alex Wayman and Choong Mun-keat hold the full list to be early, and Bhikkhu Bodhi calls the later-expansion theory “purely conjectural”:cite[wiki].]

The Four Noble Truths trace suffering to craving and teach that it can end:cite[brit]. Early discourses pair the second truth with the chain forward and the third with the chain reversed:cite[wiki]: when ignorance, the first link, ceases, each later link ceases with it:cite[wiki]. For Jitendra Mohanty, the insight that destroys ignorance takes craving and suffering with it:cite[brit-india].:note[For Peter Harvey and Ajahn Brahm not every link is enough to produce the next; Harvey notes that craving needs ignorance as well as feeling, so a buddha can feel without craving. Bhikkhu Bodhi adds that a chain in which each factor necessitated the next “could never be broken”:cite[wiki].]

::figure[Read downwards, the twelve links (nidāna) are the chain as the second truth applies it, suffering arising; the Four Noble Truths trace that suffering to craving (tṛṣṇā), highlighted:cite[wiki]:cite[brit]. The third truth applies it inversely, as cessation: once ignorance (avidyā) ceases, each later link ceases in turn, down to the still frame with all twelve ceased (dashed):cite[wiki].]{#bauddha-twelve-links}

The discourses call all conditioned things impermanent (anitya, Pāli anicca):cite[sep-abhidharma], though not yet momentary:cite[sep]. Abhidharma (Pāli Abhidhamma), the analysis of experience into elementary events (dharmas), took that step:cite[sep-abhidharma]: the Sarvāstivāda for all physical and mental events, later Theravāda for the mental alone:cite[sep-abhidharma]. A mind is then a series (santāna) of brief events, each conditioning the next, that looks continuous only because it is quick:cite[sep-abhidharma].:note[Dharmakīrti later argued for it in general: to be real is to have causal power, and nothing unchanging could produce effects, either all at once or one after another, so whatever exists lasts a moment:cite[sep-dharmakirti].]

The Sarvāstivādins (“all exists”) held that dharmas exist in past, present and future; the Sautrāntikas (“those who rely on the sūtras”) admitted only present ones:cite[sep-abhidharma]. India's two main Mahāyāna schools took their questions from Abhidharma:cite[sep-abhidharma]:cite[sep-yogacara]. Madhyamaka, the “middle way” school built on Nāgārjuna (c. 150 CE), finds nothing ultimately real: whatever arises dependently lacks a nature of its own (svabhāva), and so is empty (śūnya):cite[iep].:note[Critics, Buddhist and Brahmanical, heard nihilism in this. Nāgārjuna's answer is that emptiness just is dependent origination: only because things lack an essence can suffering arise and be brought to an end (MMK 24.20). Mādhyamikas turned the charge back on Abhidharma, whose ultimate dharmas leave no real place for the persons that ethics addresses:cite[iep].] Yogācāra, systematised by Asaṅga and Vasubandhu (4th–5th century), teaches “cognition only” (vijñaptimātra) and a store consciousness (ālayavijñāna):cite[sep-yogacara].:note[It is usually read as some kind of idealism; the dispute is over which. The traditionally dominant reading, drawn especially from Vasubandhu's Twenty Verses (Viṃśikā) and held by Lambert Schmithausen and Mark Siderits among others, is ontological: nothing exists beyond minds. Thomas Kochumuttom, Richard Hayes and others read it as epistemological, a limit on what cognition can reach, and Dan Lusthaus as phenomenology:cite[sep-yogacara]. Not-self still applies: Yogācāra does not make consciousness a substance, either one per person or a single universal one:cite[sep-yogacara].]

The pramāṇa tradition of Dignāga (c. 480–540) and Dharmakīrti (6th or 7th century, disputed) argued the Buddhist case against other schools:cite[sep-dharmakirti].:note[“Pramāṇavāda” (“doctrine of valid knowledge”) is a modern label, a “convenient misnomer” in Tom Tillemans' words, for a school with no Sanskrit name of its own; Tibetan writers spoke of “those who follow reasoning”:cite[sep-dharmakirti]:cite[sep-yogacara].] The map's two chips, perception (pratyakṣa) and inference (anumāna), are its count:cite[wiki-bphil], matching Dignāga's two kinds of object, particulars and universals:cite[sep-dharmakirti].:note[Scripture, for Dharmakīrti, is at best a fallible kind of inference, to be relied on only where perception and reasoning cannot reach, such as the details of karma:cite[sep-dharmakirti].] [Pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) compares the other schools' counts.
