Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika joins two schools that grew up apart and later became one:cite[brit]. Each began from a root text: Nyāya from the Nyāyasūtra of Gautama, also called Akṣapāda, and Vaiśeṣika from Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣikasūtra:cite[sep]. Early on, Vaiśeṣika worked chiefly on what exists and Nyāya on knowledge, logic and debate:cite[sep]. Neither founder can be securely dated; estimates for each range across the turn of the era:cite[wiki],:cite[wiki-nyaya],:cite[brit].:note[For Kaṇāda and his sūtra, Wikipedia gives the sixth to the second century BCE:cite[wiki], Ganeri about 100 BCE:cite[sep], Gillon about the first century CE:cite[sep-logic], and Britannica, with a question mark, the second or third century CE:cite[brit]. For the Nyāyasūtra, Wikipedia reports estimates from the sixth century BCE to the second CE:cite[wiki-nyaya]; Britannica gives the second century BCE:cite[brit-nyaya], Ganeri about 100 CE:cite[sep], Dasti about 200 CE:cite[iep], and Gillon the second century CE, adding that some put the text’s final redaction in the third:cite[sep-logic].]

For Nyāya, knowledge comes through four pramāṇas: perception, inference, comparison and testimony:cite[iep],:cite[brit-nyaya]. Inference was its great subject:cite[brit-nyaya]; to set one out for others, it used a five-membered form:cite[iep]. Its stock example proves fire on a hill: the thesis (pratijñā) says the hill has fire; the reason (hetu), that it has smoke; the example (udāharaṇa) gives the rule, wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen hearth, while a lake has neither; the application (upanaya) says this hill has such smoke; and the conclusion (nigamana), that the hill therefore has fire:cite[iep],:cite[wiki-nyaya].:note[Gillon traces the first known instance of the five-part argument to medicine: the Caraka-saṃhitā, a classical medical treatise, whose five parts share four names with the Nyāyasūtra’s. Its sample argues by analogy that the soul is eternal because, like space, it is unproduced:cite[sep-logic]. The Nyāyasūtra gives no example of its own; Nyāya’s earliest ones are reconstructed from the Nyāyabhāṣya ascribed to Vātsyāyana (fifth century), and they too argue by analogy:cite[sep-logic].]

::figure[Nyāya’s five-membered argument, read from top to bottom, with its stock example of fire on a hill:cite[iep],:cite[wiki-nyaya].]{#nyaya-five-members}

Vaiśeṣika classifies all that exists into categories, padārthas, a term formed from pada, word, and artha, meaning:cite[sep]. The Vaiśeṣikasūtra already has six: substance (dravya), quality (guṇa), motion or action (karma), universal (sāmānya), differentiator (viśeṣa) and inherence (samavāya):cite[sep].:note[Nyāya used the word for a list of its own, sixteen padārthas, and placed Vaiśeṣika’s categories under the second, prameya, the objects of knowledge:cite[wiki-nyaya]. The Nyāyasūtra opens with this list, which runs from knowledge and its objects to the conduct of debate:cite[iep].] A seventh, absence (abhāva), was added later:cite[brit]. Wikipedia, following Radhakrishnan, credits it to the later Vaiśeṣika authors Śrīdhara, Udayana and Śivāditya:cite[wiki]; Ganeri credits the later, merged school:cite[sep].:note[The two accounts overlap: Śivāditya already presents Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika as one system:cite[wiki].]

::figure[Vaiśeṣika’s categories: the six of the Vaiśeṣikasūtra:cite[sep], and absence, drawn dashed, which Wikipedia credits to later Vaiśeṣika authors:cite[wiki].]{#vaisesika-padarthas}

Vaiśeṣika is also an atomism:cite[brit], which Nyāya shares:cite[iep]. Vaiśeṣika holds that earth, water, fire and air come both as partless atoms (paramāṇu) and as wholes built from them:cite[sep]. In this account, two atoms join as a dyad (dvyaṇuka) and three dyads as a triad (tryaṇuka), the smallest visible thing, like a mote in a sunbeam:cite[sep],:cite[dipika]. Annaṃbhaṭṭa, in the Dīpikā on his Tarkasaṅgraha, stops division at the atom to avoid an endless regress:cite[dipika].:note[A later commentary in the same e-text spells the regress out: with no last part, Mount Meru, the vast world-mountain, and a mustard seed would be the same size:cite[dipika]. Ganeri gives this as the manual’s own answer and suggests it begs the question, since it takes a whole’s size to depend on the size, number and arrangement of its parts, which only an atomist need grant:cite[sep].]

::figure[In the Vaiśeṣika account, two atoms join as a dyad and three dyads as a triad, the smallest visible thing:cite[sep],:cite[dipika].]{#vaisesika-atoms}

Sources date the merger differently. Britannica says the two “were often combined” from about the tenth century and had fully fused by the eleventh:cite[brit-nyaya],:cite[brit]. Ganeri puts the merger in the eleventh or twelfth century and calls the merged school [Navya-Nyāya](/navya-nyaya):cite[sep]; Dasti and Wikipedia keep that name for the new phase that begins with Gaṅgeśa in the fourteenth century, after a syncretist period:cite[iep],:cite[wiki-nyaya].:note[Udayana wrote commentaries in both lines:cite[wiki-nyaya],:cite[wiki], and Varadarāja’s twelfth-century Tārkikarakṣā is a work of the syncretist school:cite[wiki-nyaya].] Nyāya adopted Vaiśeṣika's categories, with some changes, and kept its own theory of knowledge:cite[iep],:cite[sep]. The schools had differed on the pramāṇas, classical Vaiśeṣika accepting only perception and inference:cite[wiki]; the merged school kept Nyāya's four:cite[sep]. The map's chips follow Nyāya, as [pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) explains.
