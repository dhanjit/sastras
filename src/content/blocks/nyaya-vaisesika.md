Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika joins two schools that grew up apart and later became one:cite[brit]. Each began from a root text: Nyāya from the Nyāyasūtra of Gautama, also called Akṣapāda, and Vaiśeṣika from Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣikasūtra:cite[sep]. Early on, Vaiśeṣika worked chiefly on what exists and Nyāya on knowledge, logic and debate:cite[sep]. Neither founder can be securely dated; estimates for each range across the turn of the era:cite[wiki],:cite[wiki-nyaya],:cite[brit].:note[For Kaṇāda and his sūtra, Wikipedia gives the sixth to the second century BCE:cite[wiki], Ganeri about 100 BCE:cite[sep], Gillon about the first century CE:cite[sep-logic], and Britannica, with a question mark, the second or third century CE:cite[brit]. For the Nyāyasūtra, Wikipedia reports estimates from the sixth century BCE to the second CE:cite[wiki-nyaya]; Britannica gives the second century BCE:cite[brit-nyaya], Ganeri about 100 CE:cite[sep], Dasti about 200 CE:cite[iep], and Gillon the second century CE, adding that some put the text’s final redaction in the third:cite[sep-logic].]

For Nyāya, knowledge comes through four pramāṇas: perception, inference, comparison and testimony:cite[iep],:cite[brit-nyaya]. Inference was its great subject:cite[brit-nyaya], and to set one out for others it used a five-membered argument:cite[iep]. Its stock example proves fire on a hill: the thesis (pratijñā) says the hill has fire; the reason (hetu), that it has smoke; the example (udāharaṇa) gives the rule, wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen hearth, while a lake has neither; the application (upanaya) says this hill has such smoke; and the conclusion (nigamana), that the hill therefore has fire:cite[iep],:cite[wiki-nyaya].:note[Nyāya was not alone in using five parts. India’s earliest medical text, the Caraka-saṃhitā, uses them too, with four of the five names the same, and unlike the Nyāyasūtra it gives a worked example; Nyāya’s earliest examples have to be reconstructed from Vātsyāyana’s fifth-century Nyāyabhāṣya, its oldest surviving commentary:cite[sep-logic].]

Vaiśeṣika classifies all that exists into categories, padārthas, a term formed from pada, word, and artha, meaning:cite[sep]. The Vaiśeṣikasūtra already has six: substance (dravya), quality (guṇa), motion or action (karma), universal (sāmānya), differentiator (viśeṣa) and inherence (samavāya):cite[sep]. A seventh, absence (abhāva), was added later:cite[brit]. Wikipedia, following Radhakrishnan, credits it to the Vaiśeṣika authors Śrīdhara, Udayana and Śivāditya:cite[wiki]; Ganeri calls absence a distinctive addition of the later school, Navya-Nyāya:cite[sep].:note[Nyāya used the word for a list of its own, sixteen padārthas, and placed Vaiśeṣika’s categories under the second, prameya, the objects of knowledge:cite[wiki-nyaya]. The Nyāyasūtra opens with this list, which runs from knowledge and its objects to the conduct of debate:cite[iep].]

Vaiśeṣika is also an atomism:cite[brit], which Nyāya came to share:cite[iep]. On the conventional Vaiśeṣika account, earth, water, fire and air each exist both as indivisible atoms (paramāṇu) and as composites:cite[sep]. Two atoms make a dyad (dvyaṇuka) and three dyads a triad (tryaṇuka); a triad, such as a speck of dust in a sunbeam, is the smallest visible thing:cite[sep],:cite[wiki]. A later manual, Annaṃbhaṭṭa's Tarkasaṅgraha, argues that division must stop somewhere, or Mount Meru and a mustard seed, each with endless parts, would be the same size:cite[sep].:note[Ganeri suggests the argument begs the question: it takes a whole’s size to depend on the size, number and arrangement of its parts, which only an atomist need grant:cite[sep].]

Sources date the merger differently. Britannica has the schools often combined from about the tenth century and fully fused by the eleventh:cite[brit-nyaya],:cite[brit]; Ganeri places it in the eleventh or twelfth century and has it produce [Navya-Nyāya](/navya-nyaya):cite[sep]. Nyāya adopted Vaiśeṣika's categories, with some changes, and kept its own theory of knowledge:cite[iep],:cite[sep]. Udayana wrote commentaries in both lines:cite[wiki-nyaya],:cite[wiki], and Varadarāja's twelfth-century Tārkikarakṣā is a work of the syncretist school:cite[wiki-nyaya]. The pramāṇas remained a difference, for classical Vaiśeṣika accepted only perception and inference:cite[wiki]. The map's chips follow Nyāya, as [pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) explains.
