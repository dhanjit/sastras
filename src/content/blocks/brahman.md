Brahman is what the [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) hold to be ultimately real:cite[brit]: eternal and conscious, infinite and everywhere, underlying a world of change:cite[brit], the source from which everything arises and into which it is withdrawn:cite[wiki]. The word is older than this sense: in the hymns Jan Gonda takes to be the oldest, it is the power in the Veda's sounds, words and formulas, though not even there its only meaning:cite[wiki].:note[The etymology is uncertain:cite[brit]; one proposed root is bṛh, “to grow, swell”:cite[wiki]. The neuter brahman is not the masculine Brahmā, the creator god, nor a brāhmaṇa, which is a priest or a ritual text:cite[wiki].]

[Advaita](/advaita-vedanta) speaks of it on two levels: as it is, brahman is without qualities (nirguṇa), while met within the world it is brahman with qualities (saguṇa), the personal Lord, īśvara:cite[iep]. Śaṅkara treats the Lord as the lower brahman; in his final view only brahman without parts or attributes remains:cite[sep-sankara]. Advaita calls it pure being, consciousness and bliss, and holds that consciousness is not something brahman has but what it is:cite[iep].:note[Sat-cit-ānanda (saccidānanda) joins sat, “being”, cit, “consciousness”, and ānanda, “bliss”:cite[wiki-saccidananda]. The compound is found early in the Tejobindu Upaniṣad, while older Upaniṣads describe brahman in kindred terms; theistic traditions identify it with Viṣṇu, Śiva or the Goddess:cite[wiki-saccidananda]. The schools read such descriptions differently: where the Taittirīya Upaniṣad calls brahman truth, knowledge and the infinite, Rāmānuja’s Advaita opponents take the words to state what brahman is, Rāmānuja to name its real qualities:cite[iep-ramanuja].] Rāmānuja and Madhva both reject a brahman without qualities, and both identify it with Viṣṇu:cite[iep-madhva]. Rāmānuja's brahman is Nārāyaṇa together with Śrī, free of every fault and rich in auspicious qualities beyond number:cite[iep-ramanuja]. Nimbārka accepts both descriptions:cite[wiki-vedanta].

Every school of [Vedānta](/vedanta) makes brahman both the material and the efficient cause of the world:cite[wiki-advaita]. Andrew Nicholson finds the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra) taking the world to be a real transformation (pariṇāma) of brahman, the view most early Vedāntins seem to have held:cite[wiki-vedanta]; most later schools keep it:cite[wiki-advaita]. Advaita came to hold that brahman appears as the world without changing at all, through [māyā](/maya):cite[iep]; Paul Hacker, Hajime Nakamura and Nicholson argue that this view, vivarta, was not Śaṅkara's own but took shape after him:cite[wiki-advaita]. For Rāmānuja, souls and matter are real and distinct, and stand to brahman as a body to its soul:cite[iep-ramanuja]. For Madhva, brahman alone is independent; souls and matter are real, depend on it, and are forever other than it:cite[iep-madhva]. The Bhedābheda tradition holds the world both different and not different from brahman:cite[brit].

The same split runs through their accounts of the self ([ātman](/atman)): one with brahman for Advaita, a mode of brahman for Rāmānuja, eternally distinct for Madhva:cite[wiki-vedanta]. Advaita is the best known of these schools, but Gavin Flood cautions against taking it for Vedānta as a whole, which he sees as largely a Vaiṣṇava discourse:cite[wiki-vedanta].
