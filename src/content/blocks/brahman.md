Brahman is what the [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) hold to be ultimately real:cite[brit]. For all their variety, they agree that it is conscious, eternal and boundless, the abiding reality within a world of change:cite[brit], from which all things come and back into which they are drawn:cite[wiki]. The word is older than this sense: in what Jan Gonda takes to be the oldest hymns, brahman is the potency of the Veda's spoken formulas, though even there it has other senses:cite[wiki].:note[The etymology is uncertain:cite[brit]; one proposed root is bṛh, “to grow, swell”:cite[wiki]. The neuter brahman is not the masculine Brahmā, the creator god, nor a brāhmaṇa, which is a priest or a ritual text:cite[wiki].]

[Advaita](/advaita-vedanta) speaks of it on two levels: as it is, brahman is without qualities (nirguṇa), while met within the world it is brahman with qualities (saguṇa), the personal Lord, īśvara:cite[iep]. [Śaṅkara](/sankara) usually ranks the Lord as the lower (apara) brahman; the higher (para) brahman, for him, has no attributes at all:cite[sep-sankara]. Advaita describes brahman as pure being, consciousness and bliss, and denies that it merely possesses consciousness: the two are one:cite[iep].:note[Sat-cit-ānanda (saccidānanda) joins sat, “being”, cit, “consciousness”, and ānanda, “bliss”:cite[wiki-saccidananda]. Older Upaniṣads describe brahman in kindred terms, and the compound itself occurs in the Tejobindu Upaniṣad (3.11), which some date before the fourth century CE:cite[wiki-saccidananda]; Richard King, though, treats sat-cit-ānanda as a predicate that came after Śaṅkara:cite[wiki-advaita]. Theistic traditions identify it with Viṣṇu, Śiva or the Goddess:cite[wiki-saccidananda]. The schools read such descriptions differently: where the Taittirīya Upaniṣad calls brahman truth, knowledge and the infinite, Rāmānuja’s Advaita opponents take the words to state what brahman is, Rāmānuja to name its real qualities:cite[iep-ramanuja].] Rāmānuja and Madhva both reject a brahman without qualities, and both identify it with Viṣṇu:cite[iep-madhva]. Rāmānuja's brahman is Nārāyaṇa together with Śrī, free of every fault and rich in auspicious qualities beyond number:cite[iep-ramanuja].

Every school of [Vedānta](/vedanta) except Madhva's makes brahman both the material and the efficient cause of the world; for Dvaita it is the efficient cause alone:cite[wiki-vedanta]. Andrew Nicholson finds the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra) itself treating the world as a real transformation (pariṇāma) of brahman, “the view most common among early Vedantins”:cite[wiki-vedanta]; most later schools keep it:cite[wiki-advaita]. Advaita holds instead that brahman appears as the world through [māyā](/maya) without changing at all:cite[iep]; how far Śaṅkara himself held this is disputed:cite[sep-sankara]:cite[wiki-advaita]. For Rāmānuja, selves ([ātman](/atman)) and matter are real and distinct, and stand to brahman as a body to its soul:cite[iep-ramanuja]. For Madhva, brahman alone is independent; selves and matter are real, depend on it, and are forever other than it:cite[iep-madhva]. The Bhedābheda tradition holds the world both different and not different from brahman:cite[brit].
