What in a person is really “I”? The [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) return to the question again and again:cite[sep], and name the answer ātman, a word that in the early Vedas is mostly a plain reflexive, “oneself”:cite[brit]. Yājñavalkya, in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, describes a self that does all the seeing and knowing but can never itself be seen or known:cite[sep]; the same text also has a self that becomes good or bad through its deeds:cite[sep]. Some passages identify it with brahman, others make it a part of brahman:cite[wiki].

The orthodox schools all accept a self:cite[wiki], but split over whether it has consciousness or is consciousness:cite[sep]. For Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika there are many selves, each a substance with consciousness among its qualities:cite[sep]. The Nyāyasūtra (1.1.10) gives its signs as desire, aversion, volition, pleasure, pain and cognition:cite[sep]. These need the self's link with a mind and body, so it lacks them in deep sleep:cite[wiki] and loses them for good at liberation:cite[sep].

[Advaita](/advaita-vedanta) holds that the self is consciousness itself, and one with brahman:cite[sep]; selves look many only as the space inside many pots looks divided:cite[iep-advaita]. Rāmānuja's Viśiṣṭādvaita keeps selves real and plural:cite[iep-ramanuja]. With matter they are brahman's body, and brahman indwells them as a soul its body:cite[iep-ramanuja]. Madhva's Dvaita makes the difference permanent: every self depends on brahman and so is other than it:cite[iep-madhva], never becomes one with God:cite[wiki], and is released only by Viṣṇu's grace:cite[iep-madhva].

Buddhists deny a lasting self:cite[brit-anatta]. Their teaching of not-self (anātman, Pāli anattā) analyses a person into five changing aggregates (skandha):cite[brit-anatta], in Vincent Eltschinger's rendering corporeality, sensation, ideation, conditioning factors and cognition:cite[sep]. Any feeling or consciousness, the Buddha teaches, should be seen thus: “This is not mine. This is not my self” (Majjhima Nikāya I 130):cite[sep-buddhism]. The teaching is meant as a cure: grasping at “I” and “mine” feeds craving and ill will, and seeing through it is part of the path to nirvāṇa:cite[sep-buddhism]. Karma and rebirth remain, but no self passes from life to life:cite[sep]; Buddhists call this a middle way between an eternal self and annihilation at death:cite[sep-buddhism].:note[Buddhists argued among themselves. The Pudgalavādins (“personalists”) held that the person (pudgala), though not a self, is real; for Abhidharma thinkers it was only a name for the aggregates, and Vasubandhu turned the same argument against the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika self:cite[sep]. Caroline Rhys Davids, Isaline Horner and Kamaleswar Bhattacharya read some early passages as leaving room for a deeper self:cite[sep-buddhism], and Johannes Bronkhorst thinks early Buddhism may not have denied a soul:cite[wiki]. Christian Coseru reports that the prevailing view finds such readings tenuous:cite[sep-buddhism].]
