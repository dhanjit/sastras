What in a person is really “I”? The [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) keep returning to the question:cite[sep], and name the answer ātman, a word that in the early Vedas is mostly a plain reflexive, “oneself”:cite[brit]. Yājñavalkya, in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, describes a self that does all the seeing and knowing but can never itself be seen or known:cite[sep]; the same text also has a self that becomes good or bad through its deeds:cite[sep]. Whether it simply is brahman or only a part of it, the Upaniṣads do not agree:cite[wiki].

The orthodox schools all accept a self:cite[wiki], but split over whether it has consciousness or is consciousness:cite[sep]. For Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika there are many selves, each a substance with consciousness among its qualities:cite[sep]. The Nyāyasūtra (1.1.10) gives its signs as desire, aversion, volition, pleasure, pain and cognition:cite[sep]. These need the self's link with a mind and body, so deep sleep suspends them and liberation ends them for good:cite[sep]:cite[wiki].

[Advaita](/advaita-vedanta) holds that the self is consciousness itself, and one with brahman:cite[sep]; selves look many only as the space inside many pots looks divided:cite[iep-advaita]. Rāmānuja's Viśiṣṭādvaita keeps selves real and plural:cite[iep-ramanuja]. With matter they are brahman's body, and brahman indwells them as a soul its body:cite[iep-ramanuja]. Madhva's Dvaita makes the difference permanent: every self depends on brahman and so is other than it:cite[iep-madhva], never becomes one with God:cite[wiki], and is released only by Viṣṇu's grace:cite[iep-madhva].

Buddhists deny a lasting self:cite[brit-anatta]. Their teaching of not-self (anātman, Pāli anattā) analyses a person into five changing aggregates (skandha):cite[brit-anatta]: form (rūpa), sensation (vedanā), apperception (saṃjñā), formations (saṃskāra) and consciousness (vijñāna):cite[sep-buddhism]. No sensation and no state of consciousness, past, present or future, inner or outer, should be taken as one's own or as one's self, the Buddha teaches in an early discourse (Majjhima Nikāya 22):cite[sep-buddhism]. The teaching is meant as a cure: grasping at “I” and “mine” feeds craving and ill will, and seeing through it is part of the path to nirvāṇa:cite[sep-buddhism]. Karma and rebirth remain, carried by a stream (santāna) of causally linked states rather than by a self:cite[sep]:cite[sep-buddhism]; Buddhists call this a middle way between an eternal self and annihilation at death:cite[sep-buddhism].:note[Buddhists argued among themselves: the Pudgalavādins (“personalists”) held that the person (pudgala), though not a self, is real; for Abhidharma thinkers it was only a name for the aggregates, and Vasubandhu turned the same argument against the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika self:cite[sep]. Among modern scholars, Caroline Rhys Davids, Isaline Horner and Kamaleswar Bhattacharya read some early passages as leaving room for a deeper self; Christian Coseru reports that the prevailing view finds such readings “tenuous”, and A. K. Warder, Steven Collins, David Kalupahana and Peter Harvey argue against them:cite[sep-buddhism]. Johannes Bronkhorst, separately, thinks early Buddhism may not have denied a soul, only declined to discuss it:cite[wiki].]
