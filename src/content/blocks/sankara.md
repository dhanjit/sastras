Śaṅkara, often called Śaṅkarācārya, is the most authoritative teacher of [Advaita Vedānta](/advaita-vedanta); Neil Dalal, in the SEP, calls him its systematiser rather than its founder:cite[sep]. When he lived is contested:cite[brit]. Earlier scholars dated him 788–820 CE:cite[sep]; Hajime Nakamura argued for about 700–750:cite[sep], the range Sengaku Mayeda, writing in Britannica, finds more acceptable:cite[brit]. Several Advaita monasteries keep a far earlier date, in the 6th or 5th century BCE:cite[wiki].:note[Karl Potter, working from the date of Śaṅkara’s contemporary Maṇḍana Miśra, puts him in the late 7th to early 8th century:cite[sep]. The usual limits are his replies to Buddhist and Mīmāṃsā authors of the 5th–7th centuries and Vācaspati Miśra’s commentary on his work in the first half of the 9th:cite[wiki]. The monasteries’ own records give birth years from 509 to 483 BCE:cite[wiki].]

Beyond his own writings, nothing about his life is securely known:cite[sep]. The stories come from the Śaṅkaravijayas (“conquests of Śaṅkara”), hagiographies composed centuries after him:cite[sep], many of them between the 14th and 17th centuries:cite[wiki]. Mayeda counts at least eleven, full of legend and some at odds with one another:cite[brit]. In them he is born at Kālaṭi in Kerala, renounces the world as a boy, studies under Govinda, travels the subcontinent debating other schools, and dies at thirty-two:cite[sep]. Mayeda thinks the famous debate with the Mīmāṃsā philosopher Maṇḍana Miśra may echo a real conflict between Advaita and ritualist Mīmāṃsā:cite[brit]. His own tradition makes him a devotee of Śiva; several scholars, Dalal reports, argue for Viṣṇu:cite[sep].

Tradition also credits him with founding the Daśanāmī (“ten names”) orders of renunciants:cite[wiki] and four monasteries (maṭhas), at Śṛṅgeri, Purī, Dvārakā and Badarīnātha:cite[brit]. Dalal counts four or five and calls the founding purported:cite[sep], and Paul Hacker suggested the system may begin with Vidyāraṇya, a 14th-century head of Śṛṅgeri who claimed his monastery had been founded by Śaṅkara himself:cite[wiki].

More than 300 works carry his name, and most are not his:cite[brit]. The core widely accepted as his is the commentary (bhāṣya) on the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra), Mayeda's “masterpiece”:cite[brit], commentaries on the ten principal [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) and on the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita), and the Upadeśasāhasrī (“A Thousand Teachings”):cite[wiki].:note[Even the list is argued over. Mayeda accepts every Upaniṣad commentary ascribed to him except perhaps the Śvetāśvatara’s, and the Māṇḍūkya-kārikā commentary too:cite[brit]. Gavin Flood accepts only the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Taittirīya commentaries, and the Māṇḍūkya commentary has been questioned:cite[wiki].] Modern scholars mostly reject the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi and traditionalists accept it, though its translator John Grimes thinks Śaṅkara may yet be the author:cite[wiki]; Dalal ascribes most doubtful works to later monastery heads bearing the title Śaṅkarācārya:cite[sep].

His aim was to show that the Upaniṣads, read together, teach one nondual [brahman](/brahman), which he accepted on their authority rather than as something reasoning alone could prove:cite[sep]. Later rival Vedāntins, Vaiṣṇavas among them, called him a pracchanna-bauddha, a “Buddhist in disguise”:cite[wiki]. Mayeda grants that he knew Mahāyāna well, but finds him turning it against Buddhist doctrine or recasting it as Vedānta:cite[brit].
