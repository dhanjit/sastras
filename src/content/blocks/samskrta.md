Sanskrit belongs to the Indo-Aryan branch of the Indo-European language family:cite[wiki]. The name saṃskṛta is a verbal adjective that Britannica renders “adorned, cultivated, purified”:cite[brit]. This site writes it, like every Sanskrit word, in IAST, the standard scholarly romanization:cite[wiki-iast].:note[IAST (the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration) is lossless: from the Roman spelling a reader can recover the original Indian-script spelling exactly:cite[wiki-iast]. A bar over a vowel marks it long (ā, ī, ū); a dot below usually marks a retroflex consonant (ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, ṣ) or the vowel ṛ; ś and ṣ are two different “sh” sounds:cite[wiki-iast]. Sanskrit has no script of its own; the Devanāgarī shown beside each title is the one most used today:cite[wiki].]

The language has two main stages:cite[wiki]. Vedic Sanskrit is the language of the Veda; its oldest collection, the Ṛgveda, is generally dated to about 1500–1200 BCE:cite[wiki]. Classical Sanskrit is the standardized form, close to late Vedic, that Pāṇini described:cite[brit]; it took shape around the middle of the first millennium BCE:cite[wiki]. His grammar, the subject of [Vyākaraṇa](/vyakarana), made it the leading language of learning in India for some two thousand years:cite[wiki].

Its reach suited it to the śāstras: unlike the regional vernaculars, it was understood across the subcontinent, connecting scholars from places as far apart as Kashmir and Tamil Nadu:cite[wiki]. The schools of philosophy wrote their treatises in it, as did authors on logic, astronomy and mathematics:cite[brit]. The SEP calls it the “pan-Subcontinent intellectual language” in which the rival schools engaged one another:cite[sep].

Sanskrit was never the only learned language:cite[wiki]. Early Buddhists and Jains used Prakrits, the vernaculars that coexisted with Sanskrit: Pāli in Theravāda Buddhism and Ardhamāgadhī among the Jains:cite[wiki]. Sanskrit had only a limited role in Theravāda:cite[wiki]. Later, Mahāyāna Buddhists such as Nāgārjuna (c. 200 CE) and Jain authors such as Umāsvāti, in his Tattvārthasūtra, wrote philosophy in Sanskrit too:cite[wiki]. Britannica notes that Buddhist writing in Sanskrit is mainly Mahāyāna:cite[brit].
