Sanskrit belongs to the Indo-Aryan branch of the Indo-European language family:cite[wiki]. The name saṃskṛta is a verbal adjective:cite[wiki], which Britannica renders “adorned, cultivated, purified”:cite[brit]. This site writes it, like every Sanskrit word, in IAST, the standard scholarly romanisation:cite[wiki-iast].:note[IAST (the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration) is lossless: from the Roman spelling a reader can recover the original Indian-script spelling exactly:cite[wiki-iast]. A bar over a vowel marks it long (ā, ī, ū); a dot below usually marks a retroflex consonant (ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, ṣ) or the vowel ṛ; ś and ṣ are two different “sh” sounds:cite[wiki-iast]. Sanskrit has no script of its own; the Devanāgarī shown beside each title is the one most used today:cite[wiki].]

Scholars distinguish two stages, Vedic and Classical:cite[brit]. Vedic Sanskrit is the language of the Veda; its oldest collection, the Ṛgveda, is generally dated to about 1500–1200 BCE:cite[wiki]. Classical Sanskrit is the standardised form, close to late Vedic, that Pāṇini described:cite[brit]; it took shape around the middle of the first millennium BCE:cite[wiki]. His grammar, the subject of [Vyākaraṇa](/vyakarana), set the standard that Sanskrit followed from then on:cite[wiki]. For some two thousand years it remained a pre-eminent language of learning in India:cite[wiki].

Its reach suited it to the śāstras: each spoken language was understood only in its own region, but Sanskrit was shared by the learned across the subcontinent, from Kashmir to Tamil Nadu:cite[wiki]. Philosophers of the various schools wrote their treatises in it, and so did mathematicians, astronomers and logicians:cite[brit]. The SEP calls it the “pan-Subcontinent intellectual language” in which the rival schools engaged one another:cite[sep].

Sanskrit had rivals as a language of learning:cite[wiki]. Early Buddhists and Jains used Prakrits, Middle Indo-Aryan languages that coexisted with Sanskrit: Pāli in Theravāda Buddhism, where Sanskrit kept only a limited role, and Ardhamāgadhī among the Jains:cite[wiki]. Later, Mahāyāna scholars came to favour Sanskrit or a hybrid form of it, and Nāgārjuna (c. 200 CE) wrote in it:cite[wiki]; so did Jain philosophers such as Umāsvāti, in his Tattvārthasūtra:cite[wiki].
