Sāhitya, also called alaṅkāraśāstra, is the Sanskrit study of how poetry and drama are made, how they mean and what they do to an audience:cite[sep]:cite[inflib-poetics].:note[Thiagarajan ties the name sāhitya to the field’s insistence that word and meaning belong together:cite[inflib-poetics]. Bhāmaha himself defines poetry as word and meaning joined (śabdārthau sahitau):cite[bhamaha-text].] The other name comes from alaṅkāra, “ornament”: the figures of speech it first classified:cite[sep]:cite[inflib-poetics]. Malcolm Keating treats it as a discipline apart from grammar and the philosophical schools (darśanas):cite[sep].

Its theory of rasa, a work’s “taste”, is first set out in the oldest surviving text on it, the Nāṭyaśāstra, ascribed to Bharata:cite[wiki-rasa]:cite[brit]. When it was written is unknown:cite[wiki-natya].:note[Citing other works, most scholars put a first complete version at 200 BCE–200 CE (range 500 BCE–500 CE); P. V. Kane thinks parts were changed or added up to the 8th century:cite[wiki-natya]. Britannica dates Bharata to the 1st century BCE–3rd century CE:cite[brit].] The prose exposition of its sixth chapter opens with the rasa-sūtra, vibhāvānubhāvavyabhicārisaṃyogād rasaniṣpattiḥ:cite[natya]:cite[gnoli]. In Daniel Meyer-Dinkgräfe’s translation, rasa is “produced from a combination of Determinants (vibhava), Consequents (anubhava) and Transitory States (vyabhicaribhava)”:cite[wiki-rasa].:note[The determinants (vibhāva) are a feeling’s causes, both its object and whatever kindles it; the consequents (anubhāva) are the effects it shows; the transitory states (vyabhicāribhāva) are brief feelings that rise and fade around a lasting mood:cite[wiki-rasa], thirty-three in Bharata’s count:cite[wiki-natya]. As spices combine into one flavour, the text explains, so a stable emotion (sthāyibhāva) joined by these becomes a rasa:cite[natya]:cite[wiki-natya].]

::figure[The rasa-sūtra as a process: determinants (vibhāva), consequents (anubhāva) and transitory states (vyabhicāribhāva) combine (saṃyoga), and rasa arises (niṣpatti):cite[natya]:cite[wiki-rasa]. What that last step means is where the commentators divide:cite[inflib].]{#sahitya-rasa-sutra}

Its chapter on rasa names eight:cite[natya]:cite[wiki-rasa].:note[Love (śṛṅgāra), mirth (hāsya), compassion (karuṇa), fury (raudra), heroism (vīra), terror (bhayānaka), disgust (bībhatsa) and wonder (adbhuta):cite[natya]:cite[wiki-rasa].] A ninth, the tranquil (śānta), came later: Raghavan, sure Bharata named only eight, judges the śānta passages in some recensions to be interpolations, and finds Udbhaṭa (late 8th century) the earliest known writer on poetics to count nine:cite[raghavan]:cite[wiki-santa]. Later theorists largely adopted it, but dramaturgists such as Dhanañjaya rejected it for the stage:cite[raghavan].:note[Accounts differ. Britannica’s list of Bharata’s rasas already has nine, the quietistic among them:cite[brit], and the Sanskrit text cited here prints a śānta passage in square brackets, ending “thus nine rasas”, before a closing verse that still counts eight:cite[natya]. Raghavan cites an earlier list of nine, with praśānta, in the Jain Anuyogadvārasūtra (5th century, per Winternitz):cite[raghavan].]

The sūtra leaves open what niṣpatti, “arising”, means, and its four best-known readers differ:cite[inflib]:cite[gnoli]. For Bhaṭṭa Lollaṭa rasa is produced, chiefly in the character portrayed; for Śaṅkuka the spectator infers it from the actor’s imitation; for Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka it is enjoyed; for Abhinavagupta it is manifested by suggestion, waking impressions latent in the spectator:cite[inflib]:cite[gnoli]. The first three works are lost, known mainly through Abhinavagupta, whose reading prevailed:cite[inflib]:cite[gnoli].:note[For Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka (c. 900), words do more than convey sense: bhāvakatva generalises the poem, bhojakatva lets the audience savour it. Rasa is that savouring, an experience, not a meaning:cite[sep]. Keating warns that problems with the text make his account hard to reconstruct:cite[sep]. Abhinavagupta took over and developed his idea that the audience savours feeling made general, free of anyone’s private concerns:cite[gnoli].]

Before Ānandavardhana, theorists worked mainly at defining and sorting figures of speech:cite[sep]. By the 10th century readers paired Bhāmaha’s Kāvyālaṅkāra (7th century) and the Kāvyādarśa of Daṇḍin, whom Yigal Bronner places about 680–720, as foundational:cite[wiki-bhamaha]:cite[wiki-dandin].:note[For Daṇḍin, figures are the features that make poetry beautiful; he lists some three dozen, crediting them to earlier teachers:cite[kavyadarsa]. On their order, recent scholarship makes Daṇḍin the respondent, so Bhāmaha would have written by the early 600s:cite[wiki-bhamaha]:cite[wiki-dandin]. Keating dates Bhāmaha to the 6th–7th century:cite[sep-dates].] Ānandavardhana (c. 820–890) declared in his Dhvanyāloka that the soul (ātman) of poetry is dhvani, suggestion:cite[wiki-anandavardhana]. Beyond a word’s denotation (abhidhā) and indication (lakṣaṇā), he argued, lies a third power, suggestion (vyañjanā):cite[sep]:cite[sep-language]. On his analysis, in “the village is on the Ganges” the literal sense fails, indication supplies “on the bank”, and suggestion adds the purity the holy river lends the village:cite[sep]. Its highest use, for him, is to convey rasa:cite[sep].:note[For Ānandavardhana, suggestion, unlike indication, needs no breakdown in the literal sense:cite[sep]. Not everyone accepted the new power: Mukula Bhaṭṭa reduced it to indication, Mahima Bhaṭṭa to inference, and Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka rejected it as well:cite[sep].]

::figure[Read down the three powers of a word, on Ānandavardhana’s analysis of “the village is on the Ganges”: under denotation (abhidhā) the literal sense fails, indication (lakṣaṇā) supplies “on the bank”, and suggestion (vyañjanā) adds the purity the holy river lends the village:cite[sep].]{#sahitya-word-powers}

Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1016), a Kashmiri Śaiva philosopher, commented on both the Nāṭyaśāstra and the Dhvanyāloka:cite[wiki-abhinavagupta]:cite[gnoli].:note[After his commentary, Keating notes, attention moved from rasa as the text’s meaning to the reader’s experience of it:cite[sep].] Mammaṭa (11th–12th century) wrote the Kāvyaprakāśa, a handbook on the side of dhvani:cite[inflib-history]:cite[sep-dates]. It draws earlier theory together:cite[wiki-mammata], even excerpting Mukula Bhaṭṭa:cite[sep].:note[Gnoli calls the Kāvyaprakāśa’s fourth chapter an epitome of Abhinavagupta’s commentary on the sūtra:cite[gnoli].] In the 17th century Jagannātha wrote the Rasagaṅgādhara:cite[inflib-history]; Thiagarajan treats him as the last major theorist:cite[inflib-history].
