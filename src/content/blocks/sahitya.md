Sāhitya, also called alaṅkāraśāstra, is the Sanskrit study of poetry and drama: how they are made, how they mean, and what they do to an audience:cite[sep]:cite[inflib-poetics].:note[The name sāhitya, Thiagarajan explains, reflects the field’s stress on the bond between a word and its meaning:cite[inflib-poetics].] The other name comes from alaṅkāra, “ornament”, the figures of speech it first set out to classify:cite[sep]:cite[inflib-poetics]. Malcolm Keating counts it a discipline of its own, beside grammar and the philosophical schools (darśanas):cite[sep].

Its theory of rasa, the “taste” a work leaves, begins with the Nāṭyaśāstra, a treatise on theatre ascribed to Bharata:cite[wiki-rasa]:cite[brit]. When it was written is unknown:cite[wiki-natya].:note[Estimates run from 500 BCE to 500 CE. Most scholars put a first complete version between 200 BCE and 200 CE, and the text went on being revised and enlarged; P. V. Kane allows for changes from the 3rd to the 8th century CE:cite[wiki-natya]. Britannica places Bharata somewhere between the 1st century BCE and the 3rd century CE:cite[brit].] Its sixth chapter opens the subject with the rasa-sūtra, vibhāvānubhāvavyabhicārisaṃyogād rasaniṣpattiḥ:cite[natya]:cite[wiki-rasa]: rasa arises from the combination (saṃyoga) of determinants (vibhāva), consequents (anubhāva) and transitory states (vyabhicāribhāva):cite[wiki-rasa].:note[Determinants are a feeling’s causes: the person it is felt for and the circumstances that excite it. Consequents are its outward effects, and transitory states are short-lived feelings that serve the main mood:cite[wiki-rasa], thirty-three in Bharata’s list:cite[wiki-natya].] As spices combine into one flavour, the text explains, so a stable emotion (sthāyibhāva) joined by these becomes a rasa:cite[natya]:cite[wiki-natya].

Its chapter on rasa names eight:cite[natya]:cite[wiki-rasa].:note[Love (śṛṅgāra), mirth (hāsya), compassion (karuṇa), fury (raudra), heroism (vīra), terror (bhayānaka), disgust (bībhatsa) and wonder (adbhuta):cite[natya]:cite[wiki-rasa].] A ninth, the tranquil (śānta), came later. V. Raghavan credits its recognition to Udbhaṭa, a Kashmiri of the late 8th century:cite[wiki-santa], and the set of nine was settled only after a struggle lasting into the 10th:cite[wiki-rasa].:note[Accounts differ. Britannica’s list of Bharata’s rasas already has nine, the quietistic among them:cite[brit]. The Sanskrit text cited here prints a passage on śānta in square brackets, ending “thus nine rasas”, just before a closing verse that still counts eight:cite[natya].]

The sūtra leaves open what niṣpatti, “arising”, means, and four commentators read it four ways:cite[inflib]. For Bhaṭṭa Lollaṭa rasa is produced, chiefly in the character portrayed; for Śaṅkuka the spectator infers it; for Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka it is enjoyed; for Abhinavagupta it is manifested by suggestion, waking impressions latent in the spectator:cite[inflib]. The first three are known mainly through Abhinavagupta, who quotes and criticises them, and his reading prevailed:cite[inflib].:note[Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka (c. 900) held that rasa is not a meaning that words convey but an experience they cause. A generalising power (bhāvakatva) draws the poem’s parts together, and a capacity for enjoyment (bhojakatva) lets the audience savour the outcome as rapture:cite[sep].]

Until Ānandavardhana, theory dwelt mostly on classifying figures of speech:cite[sep]. Bhāmaha (7th century) and Daṇḍin (active c. 680–720) wrote the Kāvyālaṅkāra and the Kāvyādarśa respectively, later read together as foundational:cite[wiki-bhamaha]:cite[wiki-dandin].:note[By the 10th century the two works seem to have been studied side by side:cite[wiki-dandin]. Daṇḍin traces a poem’s beauty to its figures, and names thirty-six:cite[wiki-dandin]. Which came first has been debated; recent work puts Bhāmaha earlier, no later than the early 600s:cite[wiki-bhamaha]. Keating’s timeline dates him to the 6th or 7th century:cite[sep-dates].] Ānandavardhana (c. 820–890) declared in his Dhvanyāloka that the soul (ātman) of poetry is dhvani, suggestion:cite[wiki-anandavardhana]. Beyond a word’s denotation (abhidhā) and indication (lakṣaṇā), he argued, lies a third power, suggestion (vyañjanā):cite[sep]:cite[sep-language]. In “the village is on the Ganges”, indication supplies “on the bank”, since a village cannot stand in a river; suggestion adds the purity that the holy river lends it:cite[sep]. For him the highest use of suggestion is to convey rasa:cite[sep].:note[Unlike indication, suggestion needs no breakdown in the literal sense:cite[sep]. Not everyone accepted it: Mukula Bhaṭṭa reduced it to indication, Mahima Bhaṭṭa to inference, and Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka denied that rasa was a meaning of words at all:cite[sep].]

Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1016), a Kashmiri Śaiva philosopher, commented on both the Nāṭyaśāstra and the Dhvanyāloka:cite[wiki-abhinavagupta].:note[After his commentary, Keating notes, attention moved from rasa as the text’s meaning to the reader’s experience of it:cite[sep].] Mammaṭa’s Kāvyaprakāśa, a handbook written from the side of dhvani:cite[inflib-history], drew together centuries of earlier theory:cite[wiki-mammata].:note[Mammaṭa is dated about 1100:cite[wiki-mammata]. Thiagarajan puts him in the 11th century and Keating’s timeline in the 12th:cite[inflib-history]:cite[sep-dates].] Jagannātha (1590–1670), patronised at Shah Jahan’s court, wrote the Rasagaṅgādhara:cite[wiki-jagannatha]; Thiagarajan treats him as the last major theorist:cite[inflib-history].:note[Some put Jagannātha’s birth in 1572 instead:cite[wiki-jagannatha].]
