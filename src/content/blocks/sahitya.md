Sāhitya, also called alaṅkāraśāstra, is the Sanskrit study of how poetry and drama are made, how they mean and what they do to an audience:cite[sep]:cite[inflib-poetics].:note[Thiagarajan ties the name sāhitya to the field’s insistence that word and meaning belong together:cite[inflib-poetics]. Bhāmaha himself defines poetry as word and meaning joined (śabdārthau sahitau):cite[bhamaha-text].] The other name comes from alaṅkāra, “ornament”: the figures of speech it first classified:cite[sep]:cite[inflib-poetics]. Malcolm Keating treats it as a discipline apart from grammar and the philosophical schools (darśanas):cite[sep].

Its theory of rasa, a work’s “taste”, is first set out in the oldest surviving text on it, the Nāṭyaśāstra, ascribed to Bharata:cite[wiki-rasa]:cite[brit]. When it was written is unknown:cite[wiki-natya].:note[Proposals span a thousand years, from 500 BCE to 500 CE. Judging from other works that mention it, most scholars think a first full version likely existed between 200 BCE and 200 CE; P. V. Kane holds that it was probably altered and enlarged between the 3rd and 8th centuries CE:cite[wiki-natya]. Britannica dates Bharata to the 1st century BCE–3rd century CE:cite[brit].] The prose exposition of its sixth chapter opens with the rasa-sūtra, vibhāvānubhāvavyabhicārisaṃyogād rasaniṣpattiḥ:cite[natya]:cite[gnoli]. In Daniel Meyer-Dinkgräfe’s translation, rasa is “produced from a combination of Determinants (vibhava), Consequents (anubhava) and Transitory States (vyabhicaribhava)”:cite[wiki-rasa].:note[The determinants (vibhāva) are a feeling’s causes, both its object and whatever kindles it; the consequents (anubhāva) are the effects it shows; the transitory states (vyabhicāribhāva) are brief feelings that rise and fade around a lasting mood:cite[wiki-rasa], thirty-three in Bharata’s count:cite[wiki-natya]. As spices combine into one flavour, the text explains, so a stable emotion (sthāyibhāva) joined by these becomes a rasa:cite[natya]:cite[wiki-natya].]

Its chapter on rasa names eight:cite[natya]:cite[wiki-rasa].:note[Love (śṛṅgāra), mirth (hāsya), compassion (karuṇa), fury (raudra), heroism (vīra), terror (bhayānaka), disgust (bībhatsa) and wonder (adbhuta):cite[natya]:cite[wiki-rasa].] A ninth, the tranquil (śānta), came later: Raghavan, sure that Bharata named only eight, judges the śānta passages in some recensions to be interpolations, and finds Udbhaṭa (late 8th century) the earliest known writer on poetics to count nine:cite[raghavan]:cite[wiki-santa]. Most later writers accepted it, but dramaturgists such as Dhanañjaya rejected it for the stage:cite[raghavan].:note[Accounts differ. Britannica’s list of Bharata’s rasas already has nine, the quietistic among them:cite[brit], and the Sanskrit text cited here prints a śānta passage in square brackets, ending “thus nine rasas”, before a closing verse that still counts eight:cite[natya]. A Jain text placed in the 5th century, the Anuyogadvārasūtra, already lists nine, adding praśānta, “calm”:cite[raghavan].]

The sūtra leaves open what niṣpatti, “arising”, means, and its four best-known readers differ:cite[inflib]:cite[gnoli]. For Bhaṭṭa Lollaṭa rasa is produced, chiefly in the character portrayed; for Śaṅkuka the spectator infers it from the actor’s imitation; for Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka it is enjoyed; for Abhinavagupta it is manifested by suggestion, waking impressions latent in the spectator:cite[inflib]:cite[gnoli]. The first three works are lost, known mainly through Abhinavagupta, and his reading prevailed:cite[inflib]:cite[gnoli].:note[Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka (c. 900) denied that rasa is what a sentence means; it is an experience that sentences bring about. A power of generalising (bhāvakatva) gathers the poem into one whole, and a power of enjoyment (bhojakatva) lets the audience relish it as something beyond the everyday:cite[sep]. Keating warns that problems with the text make his account hard to reconstruct:cite[sep]. Abhinavagupta took over and developed his idea that the audience savours feeling made general, free of anyone’s private concerns:cite[gnoli].]

Before Ānandavardhana, theorists worked mainly at defining and sorting figures of speech:cite[sep]. The field’s founding handbooks are Bhāmaha’s Kāvyālaṅkāra (7th century) and the Kāvyādarśa of Daṇḍin, whom Yigal Bronner places about 680–720:cite[wiki-bhamaha]:cite[wiki-dandin].:note[By the 10th century the two seem to have been studied as a pair:cite[wiki-dandin]. For Daṇḍin, figures are the features that make poetry beautiful; he lists some three dozen, crediting them to earlier teachers:cite[kavyadarsa]. Which of the two wrote first has been argued; recent work holds that Daṇḍin was answering Bhāmaha, which puts Bhāmaha no later than the early 600s:cite[wiki-bhamaha]:cite[wiki-dandin]. Keating dates Bhāmaha to the 6th–7th century:cite[sep-dates].] Ānandavardhana (c. 820–890) declared in his Dhvanyāloka that the soul (ātman) of poetry is dhvani, suggestion:cite[wiki-anandavardhana]. Beyond a word’s denotation (abhidhā) and indication (lakṣaṇā), he argued, lies a third power, suggestion (vyañjanā):cite[sep]:cite[sep-language]. On his analysis, in “the village is on the Ganges” the literal sense fails, indication supplies “on the bank”, and suggestion adds the purity that the holy river lends the village:cite[sep]. Its highest use, for him, is to convey rasa:cite[sep].:note[For Ānandavardhana, suggestion, unlike indication, needs no breakdown in the literal sense:cite[sep]. Not everyone accepted the new power: Mukula Bhaṭṭa reduced it to indication, Mahima Bhaṭṭa to inference, and Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka rejected it as well:cite[sep].]

Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1016), a Kashmiri Śaiva philosopher, commented on both the Nāṭyaśāstra and the Dhvanyāloka:cite[wiki-abhinavagupta]:cite[gnoli].:note[After his commentary, Keating notes, attention moved from rasa as the text’s meaning to the reader’s experience of it:cite[sep].] Mammaṭa (11th–12th century) wrote the Kāvyaprakāśa, a handbook on the side of dhvani:cite[inflib-history]:cite[sep-dates]. It draws earlier theory together:cite[wiki-mammata], even excerpting Mukula Bhaṭṭa:cite[sep].:note[Gnoli calls the Kāvyaprakāśa’s fourth chapter an epitome of Abhinavagupta’s commentary on the sūtra:cite[gnoli].] In the 17th century Jagannātha wrote the Rasagaṅgādhara:cite[inflib-history]; Thiagarajan treats him as the last major theorist:cite[inflib-history].
