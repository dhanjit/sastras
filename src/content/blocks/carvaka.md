Cārvāka, or Lokāyata, is a school of Indian materialism, and rejects the Veda's authority:cite[wiki]. Ramkrishna Bhattacharya finds the name Cārvāka in philosophy only from about the 8th century CE, after older materialist schools:cite[bhattacharya].:note[Some scholars dispute the tradition that Bṛhaspati founded it:cite[wiki]. Debiprasad Chattopadhyaya reads “Lokāyata” as the outlook “prevalent among the people”:cite[wiki]. The word had other senses first, such as nature lore or sophistry, and was first used for materialism between the 6th and 8th centuries CE:cite[iep]. Bhattacharya has the school living on until about the 12th century:cite[wiki].]

Its own texts are lost:cite[iep]; its aphorisms, the Bṛhaspatisūtra, are known only from quotations, most of them in works of roughly 800 to 1200 CE:cite[wiki-sutras]. Most of what is known comes from rivals, who set out its views in order to refute them:cite[wiki].:note[Their accounts need reading critically:cite[wiki]. The Nyāya author Jayanta Bhaṭṭa, for one, casts the Cārvākas as his opponent (pūrvapakṣin) on whether inference is valid, and never names them without scorn:cite[bhattacharya-jayanta].] A much-studied account opens the Advaitin Mādhava's 14th-century Sarvadarśanasaṅgraha:cite[wiki]; it paraphrases rather than quotes:cite[wiki-sutras].

In [pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra), Mādhava's Cārvāka admits perception (pratyakṣa) alone:cite[sep]. Its reasoning: no one perceives every case, so the universal rule behind an inference is never known; testimony takes the speaker's honesty on trust:cite[sep].:note[Rivals answered that no one could get through a day without inferring, and Gaṅgeśa points out that a sceptic generalises the moment he speaks, because his words mean the same thing each time he uses them:cite[sep].] Bhattacharya assigns that view to some older materialists:cite[bhattacharya]. In his account the Cārvākas accepted inference about the perceptible world, but not about the soul, God, omniscient beings or the unseen fruit of ritual (apūrva):cite[bhattacharya].:note[Purandara, followed by Aviddhakarṇa and Udbhaṭa, drew the line by what an inference rests on: perception, which they allowed, or testimony and authority, which they did not:cite[bhattacharya].] In his view, opponents such as Hemacandra, who kept ridiculing them for rejecting inference outright, confused them with the older materialists, probably by choice:cite[bhattacharya].:note[The IEP account is similar: everyday inferences, such as fire from rising smoke, counted as probable, but none about God or an afterlife:cite[iep]. Piotr Balcerowicz calls perception-only “a standard account” of a Cārvāka thinker:cite[sep-jayarasi], and the map’s chip follows it.]

For the Cārvākas, four elements make up the world: earth, water, fire and air; space (ākāśa) and atoms, being unperceived, are ruled out:cite[sep-naturalism]. Living, conscious beings arise when the elements combine in the right proportion:cite[sep-naturalism], and perish when they separate:cite[iep]. Consciousness emerges though no element has it:cite[sep-naturalism].:note[Two comparisons are reported. Betel leaf, areca nut and lime turn red when chewed together, though none of them is red:cite[sep-personhood]; and a fragment likens consciousness to the power to intoxicate that arises from the ingredients of wine:cite[bhattacharya]. Jonardon Ganeri allows a non-reductive reading, on which what emerges is as much a part of what exists as the matter beneath it; the major interpreters call the view reductionist:cite[sep-naturalism].] The self is only this conscious body:cite[sep-naturalism]. Death ends the person, with no rebirth; alone among classical schools the Cārvāka rejected karma and liberation (mokṣa):cite[sep-personhood].

For them, this life is all there is, and the good is one's own pleasure:cite[iep]. Mādhava's Cārvāka scorns austerities, and calls shunning pleasure for fear of pain foolish:cite[wiki]. Almost every other school maligned it, Abigail Turner-Lauck Wernicki thinks probably more for the egoism ascribed to it than for its rejection of the supernatural:cite[iep]. Bhattacharya replies that the sensualist picture is the opponents', backed by no genuine aphorism:cite[wiki].:note[Some copies of a single work, he adds, carry the passages that charge the Cārvākas with hedonism, and many others lack them:cite[wiki].]

The one surviving treatise with a claim to the school is Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa's Tattvopaplavasiṃha:cite[sep-jayarasi]. It argues that no means of knowing, perception included, can be validated:cite[sep-jayarasi]. Scholars split three ways over his tie to the school: the book comes from a branch of it, he was a sceptic linked to it loosely or not at all, or he was no Cārvāka:cite[sep-jayarasi].:note[The first view is that of the text’s first editors, Saṁghavī and Pārīkh, adopted by Ruben and modified by Eli Franco; the second that of a larger group including Matilal, Solomon, Gokhale and Mills; the third that of Debiprasad Chattopadhyaya and Bhattacharya:cite[sep-jayarasi].] Piotr Balcerowicz affirms the tie, since Jayarāśi saw himself as following Bṛhaspati:cite[sep-jayarasi].:note[Balcerowicz dates Jayarāśi to about 800–840, Franco to about 770–830:cite[sep-jayarasi]. Even Balcerowicz does not take the book as a first-hand source for the school’s usual views:cite[sep-jayarasi].]
