Cārvāka, or Lokāyata, is a school of Indian materialism, and rejects the Veda's authority:cite[wiki]. Some scholars dispute the tradition that Bṛhaspati founded it:cite[wiki]. Ramkrishna Bhattacharya finds the name Cārvāka in philosophy only from about the 8th century CE, after older materialist schools:cite[bhattacharya].:note[Debiprasad Chattopadhyaya reads “Lokāyata” as the outlook “prevalent among the people”:cite[wiki]. The word had other senses first, such as nature lore or sophistry, and was first used for materialism between the 6th and 8th centuries CE:cite[iep]. Bhattacharya has the school living on until about the 12th century:cite[wiki].]

Its own texts are lost:cite[iep]; its aphorisms, the Bṛhaspatisūtra, survive only as fragments, most of them quoted in works of about the 8th to 12th centuries:cite[wiki-sutras]. Most of what is known comes from rivals, who stated its case as an opponent's position (pūrvapakṣa) to refute it:cite[wiki].:note[Their accounts need reading critically:cite[wiki]. The Nyāya author Jayanta Bhaṭṭa, for one, casts the Cārvākas as his opponent (pūrvapakṣin) on whether inference is valid, and always mentions them with derision:cite[bhattacharya-jayanta].] A much-studied account opens the Advaitin Mādhava's 14th-century Sarvadarśanasaṅgraha:cite[wiki]; it paraphrases rather than quotes:cite[wiki-sutras]. What follows is the school as they report it.

In [pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra), Mādhava's Cārvāka admits perception (pratyakṣa) alone:cite[sep]. No one perceives every case, so no inference's universal rule can be known; testimony takes the speaker's honesty on trust:cite[sep].:note[Rivals replied that no one could act without inference; Gaṅgeśa adds that the sceptic relies on general rules as soon as he speaks:cite[sep].] Bhattacharya assigns this view to some older materialists; among the Cārvākas, Purandara, Aviddhakarṇa and Udbhaṭa accepted inference grounded in perception, refusing only inference beyond the perceptible, to the soul, God or the unseen fruit of ritual (apūrva):cite[bhattacharya]. Opponents such as Hemacandra, who mocked the Cārvākas for rejecting inference outright, missed or ignored the difference:cite[bhattacharya].:note[The IEP account is similar: everyday inferences, such as fire from rising smoke, counted as probable, but none about God or an afterlife:cite[iep]. Piotr Balcerowicz calls perception-only “a standard account” of a Cārvāka thinker:cite[sep-jayarasi], and the map’s chip follows it.]

Four elements make up the world: earth, water, fire and air; space (ākāśa) and atoms are ruled out, since no one perceives them:cite[sep-naturalism]. Living, conscious beings arise when the elements combine in the right proportion:cite[sep-naturalism], and perish when they separate:cite[iep]. Consciousness emerges though no element has it:cite[sep-naturalism].:note[Two comparisons are reported. Betel leaf, areca nut and lime turn red when chewed together, though none of them is red:cite[sep-personhood]; and a fragment likens consciousness to the power to intoxicate that arises from the ingredients of wine:cite[bhattacharya]. Leading interpreters call the view reductionist; Jonardon Ganeri suggests it can be read as non-reductive, counting what emerges as part of what is real:cite[sep-naturalism].] The self is only this conscious body:cite[sep-naturalism]. Death ends the person, with no rebirth; alone among classical schools the Cārvāka rejected karma and liberation (mokṣa):cite[sep-personhood].

This life is all there is, and its good, in the reports, is one's own pleasure:cite[iep]. Renouncing it through austerities, for fear of the pain mixed with it, is folly:cite[wiki]. Nearly every other school reviled it, Abigail Turner-Lauck Wernicki judges, more for this egoism than for denying the supernatural:cite[iep]. Bhattacharya answers that the portrait of materialists as mere sensualists comes from rivals who cite no authentic aphorism for it:cite[wiki].:note[Many manuscripts of the same text, he adds, lack the passages that charge it with hedonism:cite[wiki].]

The one surviving treatise with a claim to the school is Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa's Tattvopaplavasiṃha, “The Lion of the Dissolution of All Categories”:cite[sep-jayarasi]. It argues that no means of knowing, perception included, can be validated:cite[sep-jayarasi]. The tie is contested: Piotr Balcerowicz counts Jayarāśi in an offshoot of the school, since he saw himself as following Bṛhaspati, while Debiprasad Chattopadhyaya and Bhattacharya deny he was a Cārvāka:cite[sep-jayarasi].:note[Balcerowicz dates Jayarāśi to about 800–840, Eli Franco to about 770–830:cite[sep-jayarasi]. Even Balcerowicz does not take the book as a first-hand source for the school’s usual views:cite[sep-jayarasi].]
