The Bhagavad Gītā's 700 verses, in eighteen chapters, sit inside the Mahābhārata, in its sixth book, the Bhīṣma-parvan:cite[wiki]. They make up chapters 23–40 of that book:cite[brit], or 25–42 in some recensions:cite[wiki]. The Gītā's manuscripts vary far less than those of the rest of the epic:cite[wiki].:note[Swami Gambhirananda thought the old manuscripts may have run to 745 verses, a figure a few versions of the epic support, though no such manuscript has turned up:cite[wiki]. Śaṅkara’s commentary states 700, and that count has been the standard since:cite[wiki].]

It is set at the start of the Kurukṣetra war:cite[wiki]. Arjuna recoils from a battle in which he must kill relatives and friends; his charioteer Kṛṣṇa, an incarnation (avatāra) of Viṣṇu, replies, and talks him into doing his duty as a warrior:cite[brit].

Dating the Gītā means dating the Mahābhārata, itself compiled over centuries, so estimates vary:cite[wiki]. It is usually dated to the 2nd or 1st century BCE:cite[wiki]; Britannica suggests the 1st or 2nd century CE:cite[brit], and Kashi Nath Upadhyaya argues for the 5th or 4th century BCE:cite[wiki]. Tradition credits Vyāsa, but historians treat it as composite, the work of more than one author:cite[wiki].

Three disciplines run through it, each a way to the divine: action (karma), devotion (bhakti) and knowledge (jñāna):cite[wiki]. Britannica reads devotion as Kṛṣṇa's way out of a tension between the Vedic demand to sacrifice and the late Upaniṣadic demand to meditate and know: act, but without desire for what the act brings:cite[brit].:note[Splitting Hindu practice into four distinct “yogas” is modern, Swami Vivekananda’s scheme from the 1890s:cite[wiki]. For Jeaneane Fowler the text lets no one path shut out the others; for Catherine Cornille it puts devotion first:cite[wiki].]

Śaṅkara's commentary, the oldest to survive, made it one of three sources of authority, with the [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) and the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra), that any new school of [Vedānta](/vedanta) had to reckon with:cite[wiki]. He read it for [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta): devotion cleanses the mind (citta-śuddhi) until the seeker knows the self to be brahman:cite[wiki]. Rāmānuja finds in it the personal God Nārāyaṇa, a brahman whose real parts include souls and matter:cite[wiki]. Madhva's Dvaita keeps God, souls and the world fundamentally distinct: knowledge of Viṣṇu is the prerequisite for release, and final release needs his grace (prasāda):cite[wiki].
