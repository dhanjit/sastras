For Advaita, the self (ātman) and ultimate reality (brahman) are one:cite[wiki].:note[“Self” here means not the personality but the awareness that witnesses it; Advaita holds that this awareness is mistaken for the body and mind it observes:cite[wiki].] It is one school of [Vedānta](/vedanta) among several, not the outlook of Hinduism as a whole; Richard King notes that most Hindus do not follow it:cite[wiki]. He traces its standing as the model of Hindu thought to the 14th-century Advaitin Vidyāraṇya, who ranked it above every other system, and to the Western Orientalists and Hindu modernists who followed his lead:cite[wiki].

That each of us seems a separate, limited person is, for Śaṅkara, the work of beginningless ignorance (avidyā):cite[iep], which shows itself as a mutual “superimposition” (adhyāsa): we credit the self with the features of body and mind, and body and mind with the self's:cite[sep]. Classical Advaita makes it the individual face of [māyā](/maya), the power by which [brahman](/brahman) appears as a world of many things:cite[iep]; whether Śaṅkara himself joined the two is disputed:cite[sep]. Advaita therefore speaks from two standpoints: the conventional (vyāvahārika), where the everyday world holds good, and the ultimate (pāramārthika), where there is only brahman:cite[sep].

Liberation (mokṣa) is then not something to be produced: the self already is brahman, and what frees it is knowledge (jñāna) that removes the mistake:cite[sep]. Ritual action, Śaṅkara held, can only prepare the mind:cite[iep]. The knowledge is sparked by the [Upaniṣads](/upanisads)' statements of identity, such as “you are that”, and whoever gains it is free while still alive (jīvanmukti, “liberation while living”):cite[sep].:note[Śaṅkara’s story of the missing tenth makes the point. Counting heads after a river crossing, each of ten children leaves himself out, finds nine and mourns a drowned friend; a passer-by’s “you are the tenth” gives nobody anything new, it only removes an error:cite[sep].]

The school is older than Śaṅkara, who refers to a tradition before him:cite[iep]. Its oldest complete surviving treatise is usually taken to be Gauḍapāda's Māṇḍūkya-kārikā:cite[iep], dated anywhere from the early 6th to the 7th century:cite[wiki-gaudapada]; tradition makes Gauḍapāda the teacher of Śaṅkara's teacher:cite[sep]. [Śaṅkara](/sankara) systematised Advaita in the 8th century rather than founding it:cite[sep], though Richard King finds his works overshadowed until about the 10th by those of his older contemporary Maṇḍana Miśra, a Mīmāṃsā scholar who also wrote on Advaita:cite[wiki-sankara]. His successors split over where ignorance resides: in the individual soul (jīva), for the Bhāmatī school of Vācaspati Miśra, or in brahman, for the Vivaraṇa school of Prakāśātman:cite[iep].

Rival Vedāntins attacked it from Bhāskara onward, Rāmānuja and Madhva among them:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. Vyāsatīrtha, drawing on [Navya-Nyāya](/navya-nyaya) among other schools, attacked it for Dvaita in the Nyāyāmṛta; Madhusūdana Sarasvatī answered line by line in the Advaitasiddhi, and the two sides traded rejoinders for centuries:cite[wiki-vyasatirtha].
