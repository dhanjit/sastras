Sanskrit learning placed gaṇita within jyotiṣa, which Kim Plofker describes as mathematical astronomy and astrology together:cite[brit]. Jyotiṣa was one of the six Vedāṅgas that serve Vedic ritual, and Plofker traces its classical form to a blend of Indian material with Greek astronomical and astrological models that came after Alexander's invasion:cite[brit].:note[Much of what is known of classical Indian mathematics comes from astronomical texts, and nearly every known mathematical author also wrote on jyotiṣa:cite[brit]:cite[brit-structure].] Jyotiṣa also means astrology, and Hindu astrology is still widely followed in South Asia:cite[brit]:cite[brit-astrology].

The earliest detailed account of this mathematics still extant is the Āryabhaṭīya's section on gaṇita:cite[brit]. Āryabhaṭa gives his age as 23 in 499 CE, the work's usual date:cite[mt-aryabhata]:cite[brit-aryabhata]; Roger Billard estimated about 510:cite[wiki-aryabhatiya]. Its 33 verses on mathematics:cite[mt-aryabhata] run from square roots to series, quadratic equations, a sine table and the kuṭṭaka (“pulverizer”), for whole-number solutions of linear equations:cite[brit]:cite[brit-aryabhata].:note[Takao Hayashi describes the kuṭṭaka as essentially Euclid’s algorithm:cite[brit-aryabhata]. The Āryabhaṭīya also puts π at 3.1416 and ascribes the stars’ daily motion to the Earth’s rotation:cite[brit-aryabhata].]

Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628) is the first known text to set out how zero and negative numbers behave in arithmetic:cite[mt-brahmagupta]:cite[mt-zero]. Zero is a number minus itself, and the rules are framed as “fortunes” and “debts” (MacTutor's rendering): two debts multiplied or divided give a fortune:cite[mt-brahmagupta]. A number divided by zero is left as a fraction over zero, and zero divided by zero is made zero, a rule John O'Connor and Edmund Robertson call “certainly wrong”:cite[mt-brahmagupta].:note[Later authors reworked the rule without settling it. Mahāvīra, about 830, said a number divided by zero stays unchanged:cite[mt-zero]. Bhāskara II called such a quotient an infinite quantity:cite[mt-zero], yet also had multiplying it by zero give back the original number:cite[brit-structure]. None of them reached the modern view that division by zero cannot be done:cite[mt-zero].] Bhāskara II wrote his Siddhāntaśiromaṇi in 1150, at 36:cite[wiki-siddhantasiromani].:note[Tradition often counted his arithmetic (Līlāvatī) and algebra (Bījagaṇita) as parts of the same work:cite[brit-structure].]

Plofker traces Mādhava's school in Kerala as an unbroken teacher-to-pupil line from the late 1300s to at least the early 1600s:cite[brit-kerala]. Its results include infinite series for the sine, cosine and arctangent, which gave π to 11 decimal places:cite[brit-kerala]. With the angle set to π/4, the arctangent series becomes π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …:cite[mt-madhava]. No surviving work of Mādhava's states the series:cite[mt-madhava]:cite[wiki-madhava-series]; they are credited to him in Nīlakaṇṭha's Tantrasaṅgraha (1501) and Jyeṣṭhadeva's Yuktibhāṣā (c. 1530):cite[mt-nilakantha]:cite[wiki-madhava-series]:cite[wiki-yuktibhasa].:note[MacTutor dates the Yuktibhāṣā about 1550:cite[mt-nilakantha]. Written in Malayalam, it gives proofs of these results, which O’Connor and Robertson call unusual for an Indian text:cite[wiki-yuktibhasa]:cite[mt-nilakantha].] Found again in Europe by James Gregory and Gottfried Leibniz in the 1670s, the π/4 series is sometimes called the Mādhava–Leibniz series:cite[wiki-madhava-series]:cite[wiki-leibniz-pi]. It alternates in sign and approaches π/4 very slowly:cite[wiki-leibniz-pi].:note[Mādhava added correction terms to the series, and Nīlakaṇṭha gave other series for π/4 that converge faster:cite[mt-madhava]:cite[mt-nilakantha].]

How to describe this is disputed. George Gheverghese Joseph regards Mādhava as the founder of mathematical analysis:cite[mt-madhava]; Plofker replies that such Indian techniques stayed inside trigonometry, with no general idea of a function or derivative, and Victor Katz that they were never unified into a calculus:cite[wiki-kerala].:note[Charles Whish described the Kerala series to Western readers in 1835, but his paper was largely ignored until C. T. Rajagopal and his colleagues returned to the texts more than a century later:cite[wiki-kerala]. David Pingree held that Mādhava reached his series by a route of his own, without calculus, and that reading them as calculus hides his method:cite[wiki-kerala].] Whether the results reached Europe is also disputed. A. K. Bag (1979) and D. F. Almeida and colleagues (2001) suggested they may have travelled with traders or Jesuit missionaries:cite[wiki-kerala]:cite[mt-pearce]; David Bressoud finds no evidence that the series were known outside Kerala before the 19th century, and no manuscript showing such a transfer is known:cite[wiki-kerala]:cite[mt-pearce].
