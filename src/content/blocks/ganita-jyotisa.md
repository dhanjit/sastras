Sanskrit learning placed gaṇita within jyotiṣa, which Kim Plofker describes as mathematical astronomy and astrology together:cite[brit]. One of the six Vedāṅgas that serve Vedic ritual, jyotiṣa took its classical form, in Plofker's account, from Indian material blended with Greek astronomical and astrological models that arrived after Alexander's invasion:cite[brit].:note[The mathematicians known to us were nearly all writers on jyotiṣa too, so much of the evidence for their mathematics comes from astronomy texts:cite[brit]:cite[brit-structure].] As astrology, jyotiṣa is still widely followed in South Asia:cite[brit]:cite[brit-astrology].

Among surviving texts, this mathematics is first treated in detail in the gaṇita section of the Āryabhaṭīya:cite[brit]. Āryabhaṭa gives his age as 23 in 499 CE, the work's usual date:cite[mt-aryabhata]:cite[brit-aryabhata]; Roger Billard estimated about 510:cite[wiki-aryabhatiya]. Its 33 mathematical verses run from square roots to series, quadratic equations, a sine table and the kuṭṭaka (“pulverizer”), for whole-number solutions of linear equations:cite[mt-aryabhata]:cite[brit]:cite[brit-aryabhata].:note[Takao Hayashi describes the kuṭṭaka as essentially Euclid’s algorithm:cite[brit-aryabhata]. The Āryabhaṭīya also puts π at 3.1416 and ascribes the stars’ daily motion to the Earth’s rotation:cite[brit-aryabhata].]

Brahmagupta wrote the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta in 628:cite[mt-brahmagupta], and John O'Connor and Edmund Robertson call him the first person known to have tried arithmetic with zero and negative numbers:cite[mt-zero]. Zero is a number minus itself, and the rules speak of “fortunes” and “debts”: two debts multiplied or divided give a fortune:cite[mt-brahmagupta]. A number divided by zero is left as a fraction over zero, and zero divided by zero is made zero, a rule O'Connor and Robertson call “certainly wrong”:cite[mt-brahmagupta].:note[Later authors reworked the rule. Mahāvīra, about 830, said a number divided by zero stays unchanged:cite[mt-zero]. Bhāskara II defined such a quotient simply as “zero-divided”, kept the rule that multiplying it by zero restores the original number, and in his algebra likened its changelessness to the infinite Viṣṇu:cite[brit-structure]; MacTutor’s translation calls it “an infinite quantity”:cite[mt-zero]. O’Connor and Robertson judge that none of them would admit that division by zero is impossible:cite[mt-zero].] Bhāskara II wrote his Siddhāntaśiromaṇi in 1150, at 36:cite[wiki-siddhantasiromani].:note[Tradition often counted his arithmetic (Līlāvatī) and algebra (Bījagaṇita) as parts of the same work:cite[brit-structure].]

Plofker traces Mādhava's school in Kerala as an unbroken teacher-to-pupil line from the late 1300s to at least the early 1600s:cite[brit-kerala]. Its results include infinite series for the sine, cosine and arctangent, which gave π to 11 decimal places:cite[brit-kerala]. With the angle set to π/4, the arctangent series becomes π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …:cite[mt-madhava]. No surviving work of Mādhava's states the series:cite[mt-madhava]:cite[wiki-madhava-series]; Nīlakaṇṭha and Jyeṣṭhadeva credit them to him:cite[wiki-madhava-series]:cite[katz].:note[The verses survive in Śaṅkara Vāriyar’s Yuktidīpikā, a commentary on Nīlakaṇṭha’s Tantrasaṅgraha (1501), and in Jyeṣṭhadeva’s Yuktibhāṣā (c. 1530):cite[wiki-madhava-series]:cite[mt-nilakantha]:cite[wiki-yuktibhasa]. MacTutor dates the Yuktibhāṣā about 1550; written in Malayalam, it gives proofs, which O’Connor and Robertson call unusual for an Indian text:cite[mt-nilakantha]:cite[wiki-yuktibhasa]. Victor Katz cautions that Mādhava may not have been the first to find the series:cite[katz].] Found again by James Gregory and Gottfried Leibniz in the 1670s, the π/4 series is sometimes called the Mādhava–Leibniz series:cite[wiki-madhava-series]:cite[wiki-leibniz-pi]. It alternates in sign and approaches π/4 very slowly:cite[wiki-leibniz-pi].:note[The same later texts credit Mādhava with correction terms that improve the approximation, and Nīlakaṇṭha gave other series for π/4 that converge faster:cite[mt-madhava]:cite[mt-nilakantha].]

How to describe this is disputed. George Gheverghese Joseph suggests that Mādhava founded mathematical analysis:cite[mt-madhava]; Plofker replies that such Indian techniques stayed inside trigonometry, with no general idea of a function or derivative:cite[wiki-kerala], and Victor Katz that, though some basic ideas of calculus were known in India, they were never drawn together into a calculus:cite[katz].:note[C. T. Rajagopal and his colleagues revived the study of these texts more than a century after Charles Whish’s 1835 paper on them, which had drawn little notice:cite[wiki-kerala]. David Pingree held that Mādhava reached his series by a route of his own, without calculus, and that reading them as calculus hides his method:cite[wiki-kerala].] Whether the results reached Europe is also disputed. A. K. Bag (1979) suggested traders or Jesuit missionaries as carriers:cite[wiki-kerala], and Dennis Almeida and Joseph argue for the Jesuits, granting the absence of documentary evidence:cite[almeida-joseph]. David Bressoud finds no evidence that the series were known outside Kerala before the 19th century:cite[wiki-kerala].
