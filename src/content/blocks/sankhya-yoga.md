Sāṅkhya, “enumeration”:cite[iep], and Yoga share one account of what exists:cite[iep-yoga]. For both, reality is twofold: one prakṛti, nature, and many puruṣas, conscious selves, all eternal:cite[iep]. A puruṣa witnesses but never acts; all action belongs to unconscious prakṛti, which serves the puruṣas' ends:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]. Bondage, for Sāṅkhya, is a puruṣa mistaking itself for prakṛti's products:cite[brit]:cite[wiki]; seeing the difference brings release, which both schools call kaivalya, “isolation”:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-yogasutra].:note[The Sāṅkhyakārikā pictures the pair as a lame man and a blind man lost in a forest, the lame one riding on the other’s shoulders to point the way:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-karika]. Johannes Bronkhorst finds no mention of Yoga as a school in its own right before Śaṅkara’s commentary on the Brahmasūtra:cite[wiki].]

In Sāṅkhya, prakṛti is made of three guṇas, “strands”:cite[iep-yoga]: sattva, light and intelligence; rajas, energy and passion; tamas, heaviness and inertia:cite[iep].:note[Opposed as they are, the three cooperate, the Sāṅkhyakārikā says, like a lamp’s oil, wick and flame:cite[wiki-karika]:cite[iep-yoga].] Its causal theory, satkāryavāda, holds that an effect already exists in its material cause, as a pot in clay:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-karika].:note[Ferenc Ruzsa reads the doctrine as a moderate determinism: whatever a product contains was fixed by its causes:cite[iep].] From unmanifest prakṛti unfold in turn buddhi, the intellect; ahaṃkāra, the sense of “I”; then manas, the mind, with five senses, five organs of action and five subtle elements (tanmātra), which yield the five gross elements:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]. With puruṣa and prakṛti itself they make twenty-five tattvas, “principles”:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-karika].:note[Buddhi is also called mahat, “the great”:cite[wiki-karika]. The senses are hearing, touch, sight, taste and smell; the organs of action serve speech, grasping, walking, excretion and procreation; the subtle elements are sound, touch, form, taste and smell; and the gross ones are space, air, fire, water and earth:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]:cite[brit]. Verse 3 of the Kārikā sorts the twenty-five by role: root prakṛti produces but is not produced; intellect, ego and the five subtle elements are produced and produce; the remaining sixteen are products only; puruṣa is neither:cite[wiki-karika]:cite[iep]. Britannica’s short summary has the ego give rise to the gross elements directly:cite[brit].]

Classical Sāṅkhya rests on one book, Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Sāṅkhyakārikā, which displaced all earlier ones:cite[iep]. Its date is contested, from the 3rd century CE to 540 CE:cite[brit]:cite[wiki-karika].:note[The Kārikā traces its teaching from Kapila through Āsuri and Pañcaśikha, and calls itself a summary of an older, lost work, the Ṣaṣṭitantra (“sixty topics”):cite[iep]:cite[wiki-karika]. It has 72 verses:cite[iep], though its last verse speaks of an original seventy:cite[wiki-karika]. Nothing is known of its author; Ruzsa puts him about 350 CE:cite[iep], Bryant dates the text to the 4th or 5th century:cite[iep-yoga], and Britannica places him in the 3rd:cite[brit]. Gerald Larson dates the text between 320 and 540, and a Chinese translation finished by 569 sets the latest limit:cite[wiki-karika].] Proposed dates for Patañjali's Yogasūtra run from several centuries BCE to the 5th century CE:cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-yogasutra].:note[Edwin Bryant reports that most scholars favour the 1st or 2nd century CE:cite[iep-yoga]; Philipp Maas, comparing its arguments with Vasubandhu’s, argues for about 400, and Michele Desmarais finds later dates the more commonly accepted:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. Britannica gives about the 2nd century BCE or the 5th CE:cite[brit-yoga], Ruzsa about 300 CE:cite[iep]. Maas also holds that the sūtras and the commentary tradition ascribes to Vyāsa are one work by Patañjali:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. Bryant stresses that Patañjali systematised yoga practices already old in his day; he did not found them:cite[iep-yoga].] Whether Patañjali is also the grammarian of the Mahābhāṣya (see [Vyākaraṇa](/vyakarana)) is disputed: the identification first appears in Bhoja, in the 11th century:cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-patanjali], and Western scholars now generally hold them distinct:cite[wiki-patanjali].:note[Louis Renou found marked differences between the two works’ language, grammar and vocabulary:cite[wiki-patanjali]. Bryant, by contrast, would keep the traditional view until compelling evidence tells against it:cite[iep-yoga].]

Patañjali defines yoga (1.2) as citta-vṛtti-nirodha, the stilling of the mind's fluctuations:cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. Each fluctuation (vṛtti) leaves an imprint (saṃskāra) in the mind (citta) that later stirs up fresh ones, keeping consciousness turned outward:cite[iep-yoga].:note[There are five kinds of vṛtti: right knowledge, error, imagination, deep sleep and memory (1.5–11):cite[iep-yoga]. Where Sāṅkhya keeps intellect, ego and mind as three layers, Yoga folds them into one citta as its functions:cite[iep-yoga].] Where Sāṅkhya counts discriminating knowledge enough to free the self, Yoga adds a practice of eight limbs:cite[iep-yoga]: restraints (yama), observances (niyama), posture (āsana), breath control (prāṇāyāma), withdrawal of the senses (pratyāhāra), concentration (dhāraṇā), meditation (dhyāna) and absorption (samādhi):cite[iep-yoga]:cite[brit-yoga].:note[The first five limbs are outer aids, the last three inner:cite[brit-yoga].]

A key difference is God:cite[wiki]. Yoga accepts Īśvara, a special puruṣa untouched by affliction or karma, and makes devotion to him one route to samādhi (1.23–24):cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. Classical Sāṅkhya needs no god: puruṣa and prakṛti explain the world unaided:cite[brit]; Ferenc Ruzsa calls it atheistic:cite[iep]. Johannes Bronkhorst, though, finds the Kārikā itself silent, neither affirming nor denying God; commentators such as Vācaspati Miśra argue that God cannot be the world's cause:cite[wiki-karika]. Edwin Bryant adds that much early Sāṅkhya was theistic, as were later forms outside the classical school:cite[iep-yoga].:note[Bryant names the Purāṇas and the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita) as witnesses to that theistic Sāṅkhya:cite[iep-yoga]. Max Müller reports that the two schools were popularly told apart as Sāṅkhya with and without a Lord:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. The Sāṅkhyasūtra, which first appears in the 14th or 15th century, argues against God outright:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]. Earlier, the Yuktidīpikā, the weightiest commentary on the Kārikā:cite[iep], grants the Lord certain powers but denies any being who sets prakṛti and puruṣa in motion:cite[wiki].]

Both schools accept three pramāṇas, perception, inference and reliable testimony, which the Yogasūtra lists at 1.7:cite[wiki-pramana]:cite[iep-yoga]; [Pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) compares the other schools.:note[Ruzsa describes an order of use in Sāṅkhya: inference only where perception cannot reach, testimony only where both fall silent:cite[iep]. Scripture counts as testimony, but the school never uses it to establish a thesis:cite[iep].]
