Yoga takes over the account of what exists given by Sāṅkhya, “enumeration”:cite[iep], with minor differences:cite[iep-yoga].:note[How the two relate is disputed. Jakob Wilhelm Hauer and Georg Feuerstein see yoga as a practice shared by many Indian schools, its link to Sāṅkhya imposed by commentators such as Vyāsa; Johannes Bronkhorst and Erich Frauwallner think Yoga never had a philosophy apart from Sāṅkhya’s:cite[wiki]. Bronkhorst finds no mention of Yoga as a separate school before Śaṅkara’s commentary on the Brahmasūtra:cite[wiki].] For both, reality is twofold: one prakṛti, nature, and many puruṣas, conscious selves, all eternal:cite[iep]. On this view prakṛti, unconscious, does all the acting for the puruṣas, who only look on:cite[iep]:cite[wiki].:note[Ruzsa flags this inactivity as controversial: many scholars take puruṣa to be strictly unchanging, but he argues that in the Kārikā it still determines what we do:cite[iep].] Bondage, for Sāṅkhya, is a puruṣa mistaking itself for prakṛti's products:cite[brit]:cite[wiki]; seeing the difference brings release, which both schools call kaivalya, “isolation”:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-yogasutra].:note[Strictly, Sāṅkhya ascribes bondage and release to prakṛti rather than to puruṣa: in verse 63 of the Kārikā, prakṛti binds herself in seven ways and frees herself in one, through knowledge:cite[wiki]:cite[wiki-karika].]

In Sāṅkhya, prakṛti consists of three guṇas, “strands”:cite[iep-yoga]: sattva, light and intelligence; rajas, energy and passion; tamas, heaviness and inertia:cite[iep].:note[The Sāṅkhyakārikā compares the three to the oil, wick and flame of a lamp, which work as one though each differs from the others:cite[wiki-karika]:cite[iep-yoga].] Sāṅkhya's causal theory, satkāryavāda, holds that an effect already exists in its material cause, as a pot in clay:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-karika].:note[Ferenc Ruzsa reads it less literally, as “a moderate form of determinism”: nothing appears in an effect that its causes had not already settled:cite[iep].] From unmanifest prakṛti unfold in turn buddhi, the intellect; ahaṃkāra, the sense of “I”; then manas, the mind, with five senses, five organs of action and five subtle elements (tanmātra), which yield the five gross elements:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]. With puruṣa and prakṛti itself they make twenty-five tattvas, “realities”:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-karika].:note[Buddhi is also called mahat, “the great”:cite[wiki-karika]. The senses are hearing, touch, sight, taste and smell; the organs of action serve speech, grasping, walking, excretion and procreation; the subtle elements are sound, touch, form, taste and smell; and the gross ones are space, air, fire, water and earth:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]:cite[brit]. Verse 3 of the Kārikā sorts the twenty-five by role: root prakṛti produces but is not produced; intellect, ego and the five subtle elements are produced and produce; the remaining sixteen are products only; puruṣa is neither:cite[wiki-karika]:cite[iep].]

::figure[For Sāṅkhya, unmanifest prakṛti unfolds through buddhi and ahaṃkāra into two branches: manas with the senses and organs of action, and the subtle elements, which yield the gross elements. Puruṣa stands apart, neither produced nor producing:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-karika].]{#sankhya-tattvas}

Classical Sāṅkhya rests on one book, Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Sāṅkhyakārikā, which displaced all earlier ones:cite[iep]. Its date is contested, from the 3rd century CE to 540 CE:cite[brit]:cite[wiki-karika].:note[The Kārikā traces its teaching from Kapila through Āsuri and Pañcaśikha, and calls itself a summary of an older, lost work, the Ṣaṣṭitantra (“sixty topics”):cite[iep]:cite[wiki-karika]. Its text runs to 72 verses:cite[iep]; the last speaks of an original seventy, but scholars have not agreed which seventy those were:cite[wiki-karika]. Little is known of Īśvarakṛṣṇa; tradition makes him a Kauśika:cite[wiki-karika]. Ruzsa dates him to about 350 CE, later than the Nyāyasūtra and earlier than Vasubandhu:cite[iep]; Bryant puts the text in the 4th or 5th century:cite[iep-yoga], Britannica its author in the 3rd:cite[brit]. Gerald Larson dates the text between 320 and 540, and a Chinese translation finished by 569 sets the latest limit:cite[wiki-karika].] Dates proposed for Patañjali's Yogasūtra range from several centuries BCE to the 5th century CE:cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-yogasutra].:note[Edwin Bryant reports a majority for the 1st or 2nd century:cite[iep-yoga]. Philipp Maas’s case for about 400, resting partly on parallels with Vasubandhu, has won wide academic acceptance, though Bryant judges late datings problematic:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. Michele Desmarais, surveying proposals from 500 BCE to the 3rd century CE, finds the later ones more often accepted:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. Britannica offers either about the 2nd century BCE or the 5th CE:cite[brit-yoga], Ruzsa about 300 CE:cite[iep]. Maas also holds that the sūtras and the commentary tradition ascribes to Vyāsa are one work by Patañjali:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. Bryant stresses that Patañjali systematised yoga practices already old in his day; he did not found them:cite[iep-yoga].] Whether Patañjali is also the Mahābhāṣya's grammarian (see [Vyākaraṇa](/vyakarana)) is disputed: the identification first appears in Bhoja, in the 11th century:cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-patanjali], and Western scholars now generally hold them distinct:cite[wiki-patanjali].:note[Comparing the two texts’ Sanskrit, Louis Renou found them far apart in grammar and word choice:cite[wiki-patanjali]. Bryant, by contrast, would keep the traditional view until compelling evidence tells against it:cite[iep-yoga].]

Patañjali defines yoga (1.2) as citta-vṛtti-nirodha, the stilling of the mind's fluctuations:cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. In Yoga's account, each fluctuation (vṛtti) leaves an imprint (saṃskāra) in the mind (citta) that later stirs up fresh ones, keeping consciousness turned outward:cite[iep-yoga].:note[There are five kinds of vṛtti: right knowledge, error, imagination, deep sleep and memory (1.5–11):cite[iep-yoga]. Where Sāṅkhya keeps intellect, ego and mind as three layers, Yoga folds them into one citta as its functions:cite[iep-yoga].] Where Sāṅkhya counts discriminating knowledge enough to free the self, Yoga adds a practice of eight limbs:cite[iep-yoga]: restraints (yama), observances (niyama), posture (āsana), breath control (prāṇāyāma), withdrawal of the senses (pratyāhāra), concentration (dhāraṇā), meditation (dhyāna) and absorption (samādhi):cite[iep-yoga]:cite[brit-yoga].

::figure[In Yoga’s account, each fluctuation (vṛtti) leaves an imprint (saṃskāra) in the mind (citta) that stirs up fresh ones, a reinforcing loop (R):cite[iep-yoga]. The eight limbs culminate in absorption (samādhi), where the fluctuations are stilled (nirodha):cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-yogasutra].]{#yoga-vrtti-valve}

A key difference is God:cite[wiki]. Yoga accepts Īśvara, a special puruṣa untouched by affliction or karma, and makes devotion to him one route to samādhi (1.23–24):cite[iep-yoga]:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. Britannica presents classical Sāṅkhya as needing no god beyond its two principles:cite[brit]; Ferenc Ruzsa calls it atheistic:cite[iep]. Johannes Bronkhorst, though, finds the Kārikā itself noncommittal on God; commentators such as Vācaspati Miśra make the case against a divine cause of the world:cite[wiki-karika]. Edwin Bryant counters that theism was common in early Sāṅkhya and survived in branches beside the classical school:cite[iep-yoga].:note[Bryant names the Purāṇas and the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita) as witnesses to that theistic Sāṅkhya:cite[iep-yoga]. Max Müller records that the two were told apart in everyday speech as “Samkhya with and Samkhya without a Lord”:cite[wiki-yogasutra]. The Sāṅkhyasūtra, which first appears in the 14th or 15th century, argues against God outright:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]. Earlier, the Yuktidīpikā, the weightiest commentary on the Kārikā:cite[iep], grants the Lord certain powers but denies that any further being brings prakṛti and puruṣa together:cite[wiki].]

Both schools accept three pramāṇas: perception, inference and reliable testimony (Yogasūtra 1.7):cite[wiki-pramana]:cite[iep-yoga]; [Pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) compares the other schools.:note[In Ruzsa’s account Sāṅkhya ranks the three: perception comes first, inference serves for what the senses miss, and scripture is turned to only when neither settles the matter:cite[iep]. Scripture is accepted, yet the school never relies on it to prove a thesis:cite[iep].]
