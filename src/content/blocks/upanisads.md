The Upaniṣads close the Veda, hence their other name, vedānta, “the conclusion of the Vedas”:cite[brit]. Of the four genres that make up each Veda, they come after the hymns of the saṃhitā, the ritual prose of the brāhmaṇa and the āraṇyaka:cite[brit].:note[The word is often derived from upa-ni-ṣad, “sitting down near” a teacher:cite[wiki]. Patrick Olivelle, writing for Britannica, rejects that early Western reading: at first the word meant “connection” or “equivalence”, the homologies the texts draw between a person and the cosmos:cite[brit].] As part of the Veda they count as śruti, which some in the tradition hold to be apauruṣeya, “not of a man”, without author:cite[wiki].

::figure[The four genres of each Veda, read downwards: the hymns of the saṃhitā, the ritual prose of the brāhmaṇa, the āraṇyaka, and last the Upaniṣads, which close the Veda and so are also called vedānta:cite[brit].]{#upanisads-veda-layers}

More than two hundred texts bear the name:cite[wiki]. About a dozen stand apart as the oldest and most important, the principal (mukhya) Upaniṣads:cite[wiki]; lists give ten of them, or sometimes thirteen:cite[wiki-principal].

Dates are contested:cite[wiki]. Patrick Olivelle warns that no dating of the early texts is reliable to within a few centuries:cite[wiki]. In the chronology of his 1998 translation the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and the Chāndogya come first and are pre-Buddhist, of roughly the 7th–6th centuries BCE, give or take a century:cite[wiki]; in his Britannica entry he places the thirteen early Upaniṣads, those two at their head, between the mid-5th and the 2nd century BCE:cite[brit]. Scholars disagree about which Upaniṣads are older than the Buddha:cite[wiki]. At the late end, Johannes Bronkhorst, in a minority view, ties even the Bṛhadāraṇyaka to around the 2nd century BCE, near the time of the [grammarians](/vyakarana) Kātyāyana and Patañjali:cite[wiki].

They are not one system:cite[brit], and monist and dualist passages sit side by side in them:cite[wiki]. A recurring theme is brahman, a single principle behind the world's variety, which the texts locate in the ātman, the self:cite[brit]. Later theologians often treated that equation as the heart of the Upaniṣads:cite[brit], though the texts themselves describe the relation of self and brahman in more than one way:cite[wiki].

[Advaita Vedānta](/advaita-vedanta) finds scriptural warrant for the identity in the great sayings (mahāvākya), traditionally four:cite[wiki-mahavakya]. One is tat tvam asi, usually rendered “you are that”, the refrain of Uddālaka's teaching to his son Śvetaketu in the Chāndogya (6.8.7):cite[wiki-mahavakya].:note[Madhva’s Dvaita reads the phrase as atat tvam asi, “you are not that”:cite[wiki-mahavakya]. The passage itself speaks of the existent (sat), which Vedānta identifies with brahman, and does not name brahman:cite[wiki-mahavakya]. Joel Brereton, followed by Olivelle, takes it as “in that way you are”:cite[wiki-mahavakya]; Lipner grants this is technically plausible but has reservations:cite[wiki-mahavakya].]

Vedānta's three main sources are the principal Upaniṣads, the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra) and the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita), and each of its schools interprets them in its own way:cite[wiki]. Linked below are Max Müller's translations of 1879 and 1884, the first English work to cover twelve principal Upaniṣads systematically:cite[wiki].
