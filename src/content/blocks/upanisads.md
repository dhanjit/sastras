The Upaniṣads close the Veda, hence their other name, vedānta, “the conclusion of the Vedas”:cite[brit]. Of the four genres that make up each Veda, they come after the hymns of the saṃhitā, the ritual prose of the brāhmaṇa and the āraṇyaka:cite[brit].:note[The word is often derived from upa-ni-ṣad, “sitting down near” a teacher:cite[wiki]. Britannica rejects that early Western reading: it first meant “connection” or “equivalence”, the homologies the texts draw between a person and the cosmos:cite[brit].]

More than two hundred texts bear the name:cite[wiki]. About a dozen stand apart as the oldest and most important, the principal (mukhya) Upaniṣads:cite[wiki]; lists give ten of them, or sometimes thirteen:cite[wiki-principal].

No date for the early texts is secure to within a few centuries, Patrick Olivelle cautions:cite[wiki]. In his chronology the two earliest, the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and the Chāndogya, date from roughly the 7th–6th centuries BCE, before the Buddha, though which Upaniṣads predate the Buddha is disputed:cite[wiki]. Britannica dates thirteen early Upaniṣads, these two included, from the mid-5th to the 2nd century BCE:cite[brit]. Johannes Bronkhorst, who argues for later dates than most scholars accept, has even the Bṛhadāraṇyaka possibly still in composition around the 2nd century BCE:cite[wiki].

They do not form a system:cite[brit]; monist and dualist texts sit side by side among them:cite[wiki]. Many later theologies saw one equation as the Upaniṣads' core teaching: brahman, the unifying principle behind the cosmos, is found in the ātman, the unchanging core of a person:cite[brit]. The texts themselves differ on it, the older making the self a part of brahman, the younger identical with it:cite[wiki].

[Advaita Vedānta](/advaita-vedanta) finds scriptural warrant for the identity in the great sayings (mahāvākya), traditionally four:cite[wiki-mahavakya]. One is tat tvam asi, usually rendered “you are that”, the refrain of Uddālaka's teaching to his son Śvetaketu in the Chāndogya (6.8.7):cite[wiki-mahavakya].:note[Madhva’s Dvaita reads the phrase as atat tvam asi, “you are not that”:cite[wiki-mahavakya]. The passage itself speaks of the existent (sat), which Vedānta identifies with brahman, and does not name brahman:cite[wiki-mahavakya]. Brereton, followed by Olivelle, renders it “that’s how you are”, a reading Lipner accepts as technically possible, with reservations:cite[wiki-mahavakya].]

Vedānta's three main sources are the principal Upaniṣads, the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra) and the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita), and each of its schools interprets them in its own way:cite[wiki]. Max Müller's two volumes of 1879 and 1884, linked below, were the first English work to treat twelve principal Upaniṣads systematically:cite[wiki].
