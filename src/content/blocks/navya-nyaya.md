Navya-Nyāya, the “new Nyāya”, is the late form of the Nyāya school: it absorbs the ontology of its sister school Vaiśeṣika and is known for its precise technical language:cite[sep]. Its founding text is Gaṅgeśa's Tattvacintāmaṇi:cite[sep]. Gaṅgeśa taught in Mithilā, in northeastern India, in the first half of the fourteenth century:cite[sep-gangesa].:note[Dates differ: Britannica puts Gaṅgeśa in the thirteenth century:cite[brit]. The SEP also finds no clean break between “old” and “new” Nyāya; some scholars start the new school with Udayana, in the eleventh century:cite[sep-gangesa].]

The Tattvacintāmaṇi has four books, one for each [source of knowledge](/pramana-sastra) Nyāya accepts: perception, inference, comparison and testimony:cite[wiki-gangesa]. Later scholars wrote ever finer commentaries on one part of the inference book, the analysis of vyāpti:cite[sep].:note[Vyāpti, “pervasion”, is the general relation an inference rests on: where there is smoke there is fire, so smoke on a hill warrants inferring fire on it:cite[sep].] Study of those commentaries is still part of a traditional Indian curriculum:cite[sep].

In its vocabulary, an absence is analysed as something missing from a place, and the missing thing is the pratiyogin:cite[sep].:note[Pratiyogin, “counterpositive”: in “there is no pot in the room”, the pot is the counterpositive of the absence and the room is its locus:cite[sep].] A property can belong to a thing in one respect and not in another, and the avacchedaka names the respect:cite[sep].:note[Avacchedaka, “limitor”: a monkey sits on a tree's branch, so the tree both is and is not in contact with it. Saying the contact is limited by the branch removes the contradiction:cite[sep].]

Raghunātha Śiromaṇi of Navadvīpa, in Bengal, carried the school on after Gaṅgeśa:cite[wiki]. Its techniques spread to poetics, grammar and legal theory:cite[sep]. They reached Vedānta too: the Dvaita philosopher Vyāsatīrtha built Navya-Nyāya elements into his arguments:cite[wiki-vyasatirtha], and his Advaita opponent Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, trained in Navya-Nyāya at Navadvīpa, answered Vyāsatīrtha's Nyāyāmṛta with the Advaitasiddhi:cite[wiki-madhusudana].

The idiom grew so dense that 17th- and 18th-century authors wrote simpler manuals:cite[sep]. The most successful was Annaṃbhaṭṭa's Tarkasaṅgraha, with his own commentary, the Dīpikā:cite[sep]. This short book has been the standard first text for generations of beginners:cite[wiki-tarkasangraha].
