Vedānta means “the conclusion of the Veda”, and the name does double duty: it is used both for the [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) and for the school that grew out of studying them:cite[brit]. That school is also called Uttara Mīmāṃsā, the “latter enquiry”, as against Pūrva Mīmāṃsā, the “former enquiry” into the Veda's ritual portions:cite[wiki]. For Sangeetha Menon, in the IEP, “school” is a courtesy title: Vedānta is any interpretation that tries to make the Upaniṣads, or the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra) that summarises them, speak with one voice:cite[iep].

Every school must account for the same three texts, the prasthānatrayī: the Upaniṣads as revelation (śruti), the Brahmasūtra as the reasoned synthesis, and the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita) as remembered tradition (smṛti):cite[wiki]. Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja and Madhva each commented on all three:cite[wiki]. On matters beyond the reach of perception and inference, every Vedāntin takes scripture as the sole means of knowledge (pramāṇa):cite[wiki].:note[The six means of knowledge the map shows for Vedānta are Advaita’s: perception, inference, testimony, comparison, postulation and non-perception:cite[iep]. Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita accept only the first three:cite[wiki-pramana].] [Pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) explains how the map picks one branch per school.

Much is common ground: a self that acts and reaps what its acts bring, rebirth and the value of escaping it, and the Veda's authority on how to escape:cite[brit]. The schools also stand together against Buddhism and Jainism, and against the conclusions of Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṅkhya, Yoga and, in part, Pūrva Mīmāṃsā:cite[brit]. What sets them apart is the relation of the self ([ātman](/atman)) to [brahman](/brahman), above all how far the two are identical:cite[brit]. The best known are three: the [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta) of [Śaṅkara](/sankara) (8th century), the Viśiṣṭādvaita of Rāmānuja (11th–12th century) and the Dvaita of Madhva (13th century):cite[brit]. For Advaita the self and brahman are identical; for Viśiṣṭādvaita selves are distinct from God yet bound to him forever as his modes; for Dvaita they are always and wholly different:cite[wiki].

They are not the only ones:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. Bhedābheda teaches that the self both is and is not different from brahman, and that the world is a real transformation of it:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. Andrew Nicholson calls it a family of views rather than a school, since it has no one founder and no monastic network of its own; it goes back at least to the 7th century CE:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. Its teachers include Bhāskara, whose Brahmasūtra commentary defends Bhedābheda against Śaṅkara's reading, and, much later, the devotional teachers Vallabha and Caitanya:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. Scholars count anywhere from three to six or seven classical schools:cite[wiki]. Because so much of the surviving literature is Advaita, Western readers long took Vedānta to mean Advaita, a mistaken impression:cite[brit].
