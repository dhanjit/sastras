Vedānta, “the conclusion of the Veda”, names both the [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) themselves and the school that their study produced:cite[brit]. That school is also called Uttara Mīmāṃsā, the “latter enquiry”, as against Pūrva Mīmāṃsā, the “former enquiry” into the Veda's ritual portions:cite[wiki].

Every school must account for the same three texts, the prasthānatrayī: the Upaniṣads as revelation (śruti), the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra) as the reasoned synthesis, and the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita) as remembered tradition (smṛti):cite[wiki]. Śaṅkara and Madhva commented on all three:cite[wiki]; Rāmānuja wrote on the Brahmasūtra and the Gītā, and gave his reading of the Upaniṣads in an independent treatise, the Vedārthasaṅgraha (“Summary of the Meaning of the Vedas”):cite[iep-ramanuja]. On matters beyond the reach of perception and inference, every Vedāntin takes scripture as the sole means of knowledge (pramāṇa):cite[wiki].:note[The six means of knowledge the map shows for Vedānta are Advaita’s: perception, inference, testimony, comparison, postulation and non-perception:cite[iep]. Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita accept only the first three:cite[wiki-pramana].] [Pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) explains how the map picks one branch per school.

Much is common ground: a self that acts and reaps what its acts bring, rebirth and the value of escaping it, and the Veda's authority on how to escape:cite[brit]. All reject Buddhist and Jain teaching, and they dispute the other orthodox schools too, Pūrva Mīmāṃsā only in part:cite[brit]. What sets them apart is the relation of the self ([ātman](/atman)) to [brahman](/brahman), above all how far the two are identical:cite[brit]. The best known are three:cite[iep-bhedabheda]: the [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta) of [Śaṅkara](/sankara) (8th century), the Viśiṣṭādvaita of Rāmānuja (11th–12th century) and the Dvaita of Madhva (13th century):cite[brit]. Advaita holds the self and brahman identical and Dvaita holds them forever distinct; between the two, Viśiṣṭādvaita makes the self a mode of brahman, distinct from it but never apart:cite[wiki].

::figure[Self (ātman) and brahman, school by school: one circle with both names for Advaita, the self inside brahman as its mode for Viśiṣṭādvaita, the two apart for Dvaita:cite[wiki].]{#vedanta-self-brahman}

They are not the only ones:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. Bhedābheda teaches that the self both is and is not different from brahman, and that the world is a real transformation of it:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. Andrew Nicholson calls it a family of views rather than a school, since it has no one founder and no monastic network of its own; it goes back at least to the 7th century CE:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. He counts among its teachers Bhāskara, whose Brahmasūtra commentary defends Bhedābheda against Śaṅkara's reading, and, much later, Vallabha and Caitanya, founders of devotional schools:cite[iep-bhedabheda]. Scholars count anywhere from three to six or seven classical schools:cite[wiki]. The sheer number of Advaita texts once led Western readers to equate Vedānta with Advaita, wrongly:cite[brit].
