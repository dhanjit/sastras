Jains hold that their teaching has no founder, since it has always existed:cite[iep]:cite[wiki-jainism], and that in every cosmic age twenty-four omniscient Jinas (“conquerors”) teach it afresh:cite[sep]:cite[wiki-jainism]. In that reckoning Mahāvīra was the twenty-fourth and last Jina of our age, and his is the only teaching that survives:cite[sep]:cite[wiki-jainism]; he is usually placed in the 6th or 5th century BCE:cite[wiki-jainism].:note[Jain tradition dates him 599–527 or 510 BCE:cite[sep].] Jainism’s two main traditions, Digambara and Śvetāmbara, share a core philosophy:cite[wiki-jainism].:note[They differ on the canon, monastic dress and women’s liberation:cite[wiki-jainism].]

For Jains, whatever exists is either a self (jīva), which every living thing has, or non-self (ajīva): matter, space, media of motion and of rest, and in some textual traditions time:cite[sep]. Unlike Sāṅkhya’s self, the Jain self really acts, as moral responsibility requires:cite[sep]. Karma, on this view, is physical: fine matter, present everywhere, that clings to the self through passion and dulls what the self can know and do:cite[sep]:cite[wiki-karma]. Good karma binds as surely as bad:cite[iep].

To self and non-self the Tattvārthasūtra adds five more categories (tattva), tracing how the two become entangled and separate:cite[sep]. Karmic matter flows in (āsrava) through activity of body, speech and mind:cite[wiki-tattva], and is bound to the self (bandha):cite[sep]. Right conduct stops the inflow (saṃvara); what is already bound is gradually shed (nirjarā); and with none left, the self is free (mokṣa):cite[sep].:note[Pūjyapāda’s image: as streams carry water into a lake, so activity carries karma into the self:cite[wiki-karma]. Shedding is achieved through austerities, outward ones such as fasting and inward ones such as meditation:cite[wiki-karma].] Seven is the Tattvārthasūtra’s count, but Śvetāmbara Jains often make it nine:cite[sep]:cite[wiki-jp].:note[The two added are merit (puṇya) and demerit (pāpa):cite[wiki-jp]. Pūjyapāda’s Digambara commentary on the Tattvārthasūtra, the Sarvārthasiddhi, answers that both already belong to inflow and bondage:cite[wiki-tattva]:cite[wiki-ts].]

::figure[For Jains, karma flows into the self (āsrava) and is bound to it (bandha); right conduct shuts the inflow (saṃvara), shedding (nirjarā) drains what is bound, and with none left the self is free (mokṣa):cite[sep]:cite[wiki-tattva].]{#jaina-karma}

Jain epistemology rests on anekāntavāda, the doctrine that things are many-sided:cite[sep]. Take a pot being made: as clay, its substance, it already exists; as this shape, one of its modes, it does not yet:cite[sep].:note[In a canonical dialogue, Mahāvīra answers whether the soul is eternal by naming the respect: eternal as a substance, not eternal in its changing states. Gorisse reads this not as contradiction but as “making explicit the parameters” of each claim:cite[sep].] Syādvāda qualifies every claim with syāt:cite[wiki-jp], yielding seven forms: a thing is; is not; is and is not; is inexpressible; is and is inexpressible; is not and is inexpressible; is, is not and is inexpressible:cite[sep]:cite[iep].:note[Webb translates syāt as “perhaps” and notes that others prefer “from a perspective” or “somehow”:cite[iep]. Bimal Matilal reads it not as doubt but as a “conditional yes”:cite[wiki-anekantavada].] Nayavāda usually names seven standpoints, and Jain authors cast each rival school as one pushed too far:cite[sep].:note[Counts vary even within the Tattvārthasūtra: seven in its Digambara recension, five in the Śvetāmbara:cite[wiki-umaswati]. In Gorisse’s rendering the seven run from the comprehensive, synthesizing, empirical and actual standpoints to three about words: the semantic, the etymological and the actual etymological. Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika is matched to the first, [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta) and Sāṅkhya to the second for seeing only unity, Cārvāka to the third, Buddhists to the fourth for seeing only the passing moment, and the grammarians to the last three:cite[sep].] [Śaṅkara](/sankara) and other critics objected that nothing can both be and not be at once:cite[wiki-anekantavada]:cite[iep].:note[Śaṅkara makes the charge in his commentary on the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra):cite[wiki-anekantavada]. Piotr Balcerowicz sums up current scholarship: anekāntavāda gives up some forms of the law of non-contradiction, but not the law wholesale:cite[wiki-anekantavada].]

::figure[The seven predications of syādvāda, each qualified by syāt and built from three parts: is, is not and inexpressible:cite[sep]:cite[iep].]{#jaina-saptabhangi}

Digambara and Śvetāmbara alike accept the Tattvārthasūtra:cite[wiki-ts], and no older Jain treatise in Sanskrit survives:cite[sep]. Both traditions claim the man it is ascribed to, Umāsvāti, whom Digambaras call Umāsvāmin:cite[wiki-umaswati].:note[The text is dated anywhere from the 2nd to the 5th century CE:cite[wiki-ts], and scholars disagree whether Umāsvāti and Umāsvāmin were one person or two:cite[wiki-umaswati]. Marie-Hélène Gorisse dates the sūtras to about 150–400 CE, probably the work of several authors under Umāsvāmin’s name, and gives the commentary to Umāsvāti, 400–450 CE:cite[sep].] It made the kinds of knowledge into pramāṇas and split them in two: indirect (parokṣa), mediated by the senses and mind, and direct (pratyakṣa), known by the soul itself:cite[jain]:cite[sep]. Summary accounts instead count three of the usual six, perception, inference and testimony:cite[wiki-pramana], and the chips on the [map](/) follow them.:note[In the Tattvārthasūtra the indirect kind covers knowledge through the senses and mind (mati) and scriptural knowledge (śruta); the direct kind covers clairvoyance (avadhi), knowing other minds (manaḥparyāya) and omniscience (kevala):cite[jain]. Other schools use pratyakṣa for ordinary sense perception, and Jain authors writing for them produced competing classifications:cite[sep]. Later logicians counted sense knowledge as an empirical kind of direct knowledge, and Akalaṅka, a Digambara, divided indirect knowledge into five: recollection, recognition, tarka (the grasp of invariable concomitance), inference and testimony:cite[jain]:cite[sep]. Set against the usual six, that later scheme gives the map’s three chips, empirical perception, inference and testimony, while recollection, recognition and tarka have no chip:cite[jain]:cite[wiki-pramana]. Earlier canonical texts had listed four pramāṇas much like Nyāya’s, comparison among them:cite[jain], and some Jain texts add comparison to the three:cite[wiki-jp].]

::figure[In the Tattvārthasūtra the five kinds of knowledge form two pramāṇas: knowledge through the senses and mind (mati) and scriptural knowledge (śruta) are indirect, the other three direct:cite[jain].]{#jaina-pramana}
