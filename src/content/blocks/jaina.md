In Jain belief the teaching has no founder, because it has always existed:cite[iep]. Omniscient Jinas (“conquerors”) proclaim it; the surviving version comes from Mahāvīra, the last Jina of this cosmic age:cite[sep]:cite[wiki-jainism], usually placed in the 6th or 5th century BCE:cite[wiki-jainism].:note[Jain tradition dates him 599–527 or 510 BCE:cite[sep].] Its two main traditions, Digambara and Śvetāmbara, share a core philosophy but differ on the canon, monastic dress and women’s liberation:cite[wiki-jainism].

For Jains, whatever exists is either a self (jīva), which every living thing has, or non-self (ajīva): matter, space, media of motion and of rest, and in some textual traditions time:cite[sep]. Unlike Sāṅkhya’s self, the Jain self really acts, as moral responsibility requires:cite[sep]. Karma, on this view, is material: particles pervading the universe, which stick to a self made sticky by passion and cloud its capacities:cite[wiki-karma]:cite[sep]. Good karma binds as surely as bad:cite[iep].

To self and non-self the Tattvārthasūtra adds five more categories (tattva), tracing how the two become entangled and separate:cite[sep]. Karmic matter flows in (āsrava) through activity of body, speech and mind:cite[wiki-tattva], and is bound to the self (bandha):cite[sep]. Right conduct stops the inflow (saṃvara); what is already bound is gradually shed (nirjarā); and with none left, the self is free (mokṣa):cite[sep].:note[Pūjyapāda likened the activities that let karma in to streams feeding a lake:cite[wiki-karma]. Shedding is achieved through austerities, outward ones such as fasting and inward ones such as meditation:cite[wiki-karma].] Śvetāmbara texts count nine categories, adding merit (puṇya) and demerit (pāpa), which Digambara texts fold into inflow and bondage:cite[wiki-tattva].

Jain epistemology rests on anekāntavāda, the doctrine that things are many-sided:cite[sep]. On this view a pot being made already exists as clay, its substance, but not yet as this shape, one of its modes:cite[sep].:note[In a canonical dialogue, Mahāvīra answers whether the soul is eternal by naming the respect: eternal as a substance, not eternal in its changing states. Gorisse reads this as making the parameters of each claim explicit, not as contradiction:cite[sep].] Syādvāda qualifies every claim with syāt:cite[wiki-jp], yielding seven forms: a thing is; is not; is and is not; is inexpressible; is and is inexpressible; is not and is inexpressible; is, is not and is inexpressible:cite[sep]:cite[iep].:note[Webb translates syāt as “perhaps” and notes that others prefer “from a perspective” or “somehow”:cite[iep]. Bimal Matilal reads it not as doubt but as a conditional yes:cite[wiki-anekantavada].] Nayavāda sets out seven standpoints, and Jain authors cast each rival school as one pushed too far: [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta) and Sāṅkhya see only unity, Buddhists only the passing moment:cite[sep].:note[In Gorisse’s rendering the seven run from the comprehensive, synthesizing, empirical and actual standpoints to three about words: the semantic, the etymological and the actual etymological. Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika is matched to the first, Cārvāka to the third and the grammarians to the last three:cite[sep].] [Śaṅkara](/sankara) and other critics objected that nothing can both be and not be at once:cite[wiki-anekantavada]:cite[iep].:note[Śaṅkara makes the charge in his commentary on the [Brahmasūtra](/brahmasutra):cite[wiki-anekantavada]. Piotr Balcerowicz reports a scholarly consensus that the doctrine rejects some versions of the law of non-contradiction, but not the law in every case:cite[wiki-anekantavada].]

Digambara and Śvetāmbara alike accept the Tattvārthasūtra:cite[wiki-ts], and no older Jain treatise in Sanskrit survives:cite[sep]. Both claim its author, Umāsvāti, whom Digambaras call Umāsvāmin:cite[wiki-umaswati].:note[The text is dated anywhere from the 2nd to the 5th century CE:cite[wiki-ts], and Marie-Hélène Gorisse thinks several hands probably wrote it:cite[sep].] It made the kinds of knowledge into pramāṇas and split them in two: indirect (parokṣa), mediated by the senses and mind, and direct (pratyakṣa), known by the soul itself:cite[jain]:cite[sep]. Summary accounts instead count three of the usual six, perception, inference and testimony:cite[wiki-pramana], and the chips on the [map](/) follow them.:note[In the Tattvārthasūtra the indirect kind covers knowledge through the senses and mind (mati) and scriptural knowledge (śruta); the direct kind covers clairvoyance (avadhi), knowing other minds (manaḥparyāya) and omniscience (kevala):cite[jain]. Other schools use pratyakṣa for ordinary sense perception, and Jain authors writing for them produced competing classifications:cite[sep]. Later logicians counted sense knowledge as an empirical kind of direct knowledge, and Akalaṅka, a Digambara, divided indirect knowledge into five: recollection, recognition, tarka (the grasp of invariable concomitance), inference and testimony:cite[jain]:cite[sep]. Earlier canonical texts had listed four pramāṇas much like Nyāya’s, comparison among them:cite[jain], and some Jain texts add comparison to the three:cite[wiki-jp].]
