The Brahmasūtra, also called the Vedāntasūtra, works the varied teachings of the [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) into one account of brahman, in a chain of aphorisms (sūtras):cite[wiki]. Tradition names Bādarāyaṇa, in some texts called Vyāsa, as its author, though the surviving text probably grew through additions by several hands:cite[wiki]. The sūtras themselves cite earlier teachers, among them Āśmarathya, Auḍulomi and Kāśakṛtsna, who disagreed on essentials:cite[thibaut].

There are 555 sūtras, grouped into four adhyāyas (chapters) of four pādas (parts) each:cite[wiki]. The chapters treat in turn the harmony (samanvaya) of the Upaniṣads on brahman, non-conflict (avirodha) with rival schools from Sāṅkhya to the Buddhists, the means (sādhana) and the result (phala):cite[wiki].

Its date is unsettled:cite[wiki]. Proposed dates span 500 BCE to 450 CE, and Wikipedia's survey finds about 200 BCE the likeliest for a first version:cite[wiki]. Daniel Ingalls rejected Hermann Jacobi's case for 200–450 CE, while Hajime Nakamura dates the present form to 400–450 CE:cite[wiki].

The sūtras are too terse to read alone: Britannica calls them “very brief, even one-word interpretations” of the Upaniṣads:cite[brit], and George Thibaut, who translated them with Śaṅkara's commentary, judged that scarcely one is intelligible without a commentary:cite[thibaut].:note[The first sūtra is athāto brahmajijñāsā:cite[gretil], “then therefore the enquiry into brahman” in Thibaut’s rendering:cite[thibaut-sutra]. For Rāmānuja and Nimbārka, “then” means once ritual action and its results are understood:cite[wiki]. Śaṅkara denies that the enquiry presupposes ritual study:cite[thibaut-sutra]; for him it follows four qualities in the student, among them the desire for freedom:cite[wiki].]

Each school of [Vedānta](/vedanta) therefore reads it through its own commentary (bhāṣya):cite[wiki]; Thibaut found very few Hindu sects that did not want Bādarāyaṇa on their side:cite[thibaut]. Many commentaries, such as Bodhāyana's and Upavarṣa's, are lost:cite[wiki]. The oldest that survives is Śaṅkara's, as Thibaut took it to be:cite[thibaut], or possibly Nimbārka's:cite[wiki].

What divides the schools is how the self (ātman) stands to brahman: identity for Śaṅkara's [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta), a qualified non-dualism for Rāmānuja's Viśiṣṭādvaita, duality for Madhva's Dvaita:cite[brit]. In sūtras 2.3.19–28 the self is described as atomic in size; Rāmānuja takes that as the text's own conclusion (siddhānta), Śaṅkara as an objection it raises in order to answer (pūrvapakṣa):cite[wiki]. Bādarāyaṇa may have had none of these later systems in mind, and which commentator comes closest to him is very hard to establish:cite[wiki].

Among Vedānta's three starting points it stands for reasoning, beside the Upaniṣads as revelation (śruti) and the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita) as remembered tradition (smṛti):cite[wiki].
