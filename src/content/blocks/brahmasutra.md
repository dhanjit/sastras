The Brahmasūtra, also called the Vedāntasūtra, works the varied teachings of the [Upaniṣads](/upanisads) into one account of [brahman](/brahman), in a chain of aphorisms (sūtras):cite[wiki]. Its author, by tradition, is Bādarāyaṇa, whom some texts identify with Vyāsa:cite[wiki]; the text as we have it was probably built up by several hands on an earlier core:cite[wiki]. The sūtras themselves cite earlier teachers, among them Āśmarathya, Auḍulomi and Kāśakṛtsna, who disagreed on essentials:cite[thibaut].

The usual count is 555 sūtras, in four adhyāyas (chapters) of four pādas (parts) each:cite[wiki]. Commentators do not all divide the lines the same way:cite[wiki], so totals differ; the GRETIL text linked below has 545:cite[gretil]. The four chapters take up in turn the harmony (samanvaya) of the Upaniṣads on brahman; non-contradiction (avirodha), meeting objections from Sāṅkhya, the Buddhists, the Jains and others; the means to liberation (sādhana); and its result (phala):cite[wiki].

Its date is unsettled:cite[wiki]. Proposals run from about 500 BCE to 450 CE, and Natalia Isaeva finds scholars broadly agreed on the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE:cite[wiki]. Daniel Ingalls rejected Hermann Jacobi's case for 200–450 CE, while Hajime Nakamura puts the present form at 400–450 CE:cite[wiki].

The sūtras are too terse to read alone: Britannica calls them “very brief, even one-word interpretations” of the Upaniṣads:cite[brit], and George Thibaut, who translated them with Śaṅkara's commentary, thought hardly any could be understood without one:cite[thibaut].:note[The first sūtra is athāto brahmajijñāsā:cite[gretil], “then therefore the enquiry into brahman” in Thibaut’s rendering:cite[thibaut-sutra]. For Rāmānuja and Nimbārka, “then” means once ritual action and its results are understood:cite[wiki]. Śaṅkara denies that the enquiry presupposes ritual study:cite[thibaut-sutra]; for him it follows four qualities in the student, among them the desire for freedom:cite[wiki].]

Each school of [Vedānta](/vedanta) therefore reads it through its own commentary (bhāṣya):cite[wiki]; in Thibaut's day almost every Hindu sect wanted Bādarāyaṇa's authority behind its doctrines:cite[thibaut]. Many commentaries, such as Bodhāyana's and Upavarṣa's, are lost:cite[wiki]. The oldest that survives is Śaṅkara's, as Thibaut took it to be:cite[thibaut], unless Nimbārka's is older: his date is disputed, the 12th–13th century for many historians but the 7th for others:cite[wiki-nimbarka].

What divides the schools is how the self ([ātman](/atman)) stands to brahman: identity for Śaṅkara's [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta), a qualified non-dualism for Rāmānuja's Viśiṣṭādvaita, duality for Madhva's Dvaita:cite[brit]. In sūtras 2.3.19–28 the self is described as atomic in size; Rāmānuja takes that as the text's own conclusion (siddhānta), Śaṅkara as an objection it raises in order to answer (pūrvapakṣa):cite[wiki]. Bādarāyaṇa may have had none of these later systems in mind, and which commentator comes closest to him is very hard to establish:cite[wiki].

It is one of Vedānta's three starting points ([Vedānta](/vedanta)):cite[wiki].
