Mīmāṃsā (“inquiry”) asks how to read the Veda:cite[sep]. Working on the Veda's earlier, ritual layers, it is also called Pūrva (“prior”) Mīmāṃsā, as against the Uttara (“subsequent”) Mīmāṃsā, or [Vedānta](/vedanta), of the Upaniṣads:cite[sep]:cite[iep]. Its roots are the Mīmāṃsāsūtra ascribed to Jaimini and Śabara's commentary, the oldest to survive complete:cite[sep]. Both are hard to date; estimates for the sūtras differ by centuries:cite[sep]:cite[iep]:cite[wiki].:note[In Daniel Arnold’s account Jaimini is thought to have flourished near the start of the first millennium CE, and Śabara around 400 CE:cite[sep]. Shyam Ranganathan gives the 1st and the 5th centuries CE:cite[iep]. M. Hiriyanna put the sūtras in the 3rd–2nd century BCE:cite[wiki].] Later commentary on Śabara splits in two: Bhāṭṭas follow Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (c. 660), Prābhākaras follow Prabhākara (c. 700), sometimes called his pupil:cite[sep].:note[Their order is disputed:cite[wiki-prabhakara]. Tradition makes Prabhākara Kumārila’s pupil, but Ganganatha Jha thought the Prābhākara school the older of the two, and A. B. Keith dated Prabhākara to 600–650 CE:cite[wiki-prabhakara].]

For Mīmāṃsā, dharma (duty) is known only through Vedic injunction (codanā, or vidhi):cite[sep]:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]. Dharma, Daniel Arnold explains, is always still to come, so perception, which needs something present, cannot reach it; only language can, as in an injunction telling one who desires heaven to sacrifice:cite[sep].:note[The goal here is not liberation (mokṣa), which Jaimini’s sūtras never discuss; Ranganathan finds Kumārila the first of the school to accept it, beside dharma:cite[iep].]

Mīmāṃsā trusts the Veda's injunctions because no one composed them:cite[sep-epistemology]. Jaimini holds the bond between a word and its meaning to be inborn, not fixed by any speaker:cite[iep], so for the school the Veda is apauruṣeya, authorless and eternal, and no author's error can falsify it:cite[sep]. Stephen Phillips calls Mīmāṃsā atheistic, whereas almost all Nyāya thinkers make God (īśvara) the Veda's author:cite[sep-epistemology].

Building on Śabara, Kumārila argues for svataḥ prāmāṇya, which Arnold renders “immediate justification”:cite[sep]. Every cognition, he holds, is valid of itself and stands until a later one overturns it:cite[sep].:note[To demand that another cognition vouch for each, he argues, would start an endless regress:cite[sep]. His commentators read the doctrine in two ways:cite[sep]. Uṃveka granted intrinsic validity only to cognitions that are in fact true; Pārthasārathimiśra, whose reading Arnold finds arguably the better, granted it to every cognition that presents itself as true, until something overturns it:cite[sep].] Both branches, Phillips writes, hold knowledge to be self-certifying, by different routes:cite[sep-epistemology].:note[For Prābhākaras the causes that produce a true cognition also make its truth known. Bhāṭṭas infer its truth from the “cognizedness” it leaves on its object, and need an outside check only to learn that a cognition is false:cite[sep-epistemology].] Nyāya takes the opposite, extrinsic view, parataḥ prāmāṇya: to know that one knows takes a second cognition, an apperception (anuvyavasāya):cite[sep-epistemology].

Prābhākaras accept five means of knowledge (pramāṇa): perception, inference, testimony, comparison and postulation (arthāpatti):cite[wiki-pramana].:note[Postulation assumes the one thing that would explain a fact: a man who stays fat but never eats by day must eat at night. Mīmāṃsā defends it as a means of knowing in its own right, while Nyāya counts it a kind of inference:cite[sep-epistemology].] Bhāṭṭas add a sixth, non-perception (anupalabdhi), through which absences are known:cite[wiki-pramana]:cite[sep-epistemology]. The map shows the Bhāṭṭa six; [Pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) explains why.

A rite ends, yet its fruit, such as heaven, may come only after the sacrificer's death:cite[sep]:cite[edgerton]. Mīmāṃsā bridges the gap with apūrva: the correctly performed rite produces an unseen power that persists and in time yields the result:cite[edgerton]. Kumārila argued for it by postulation; Arnold finds it hard not to see it as ad hoc:cite[sep].:note[Arnold adds that it looks less ad hoc once the duty the injunctions impose is taken as given:cite[sep]. Kumārila placed apūrva in the self of the one who sacrifices; Prabhākara placed it in the act:cite[wiki-apurva].]

Āpadeva's 17th-century primer sorts the Veda into injunctions (vidhi), formulas (mantra), names of rites (nāmadheya), prohibitions (niṣedha) and explanatory passages (arthavāda):cite[edgerton]:cite[wiki]. Here prohibitions are negative injunctions; the other three count only through their bearing on these two:cite[edgerton]. Edgerton calls this a “legal logic” for a Veda read as ritual law, and Hindu law (Dharmaśāstra) borrowed its rules of interpretation:cite[edgerton]:cite[wiki].:note[Kumārila accepted the remembered tradition (smṛti) as authoritative only where it rests on the Veda, if need be on a Vedic text since lost:cite[sep].]
