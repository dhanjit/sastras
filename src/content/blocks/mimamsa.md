Mīmāṃsā (“inquiry”) asks how to read the Veda:cite[sep]. Working on the Veda's earlier, ritual layers, it is also called Pūrva (“prior”) Mīmāṃsā, unlike the Uttara (“subsequent”) Mīmāṃsā, or [Vedānta](/vedanta), of the Upaniṣads:cite[sep]:cite[iep]. Its roots are the Mīmāṃsāsūtra ascribed to Jaimini and Śabara's commentary, the oldest to survive complete:cite[sep]. Both are hard to date; estimates for the sūtras differ by centuries:cite[sep]:cite[iep]:cite[wiki].:note[In Daniel Arnold’s account Jaimini is thought to have flourished near the start of the first millennium CE, and Śabara around 400 CE:cite[sep]. Shyam Ranganathan gives the 1st and the 5th centuries CE:cite[iep]. M. Hiriyanna put the sūtras in the 3rd–2nd century BCE:cite[wiki].] Later commentary on Śabara splits chiefly in two: Bhāṭṭas follow Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (c. 660), Prābhākaras follow Prabhākara (c. 700 in Arnold's dating), sometimes called his pupil:cite[sep].:note[Which of the two came first is disputed:cite[wiki-prabhakara]. Stephen Phillips simply calls Kumārila Prabhākara’s teacher:cite[sep-epistemology]. Yet Harisvāmin’s commentary of 638 CE already discusses Prabhākara’s followers, which would put him in the later 6th century; A. B. Keith placed him in 600–650, and Ganganatha Jha held his school to be the older:cite[wiki-prabhakara]. A third school, founded by Murāri, is known only by name, since none of his works survive:cite[wiki].]

For Mīmāṃsā, dharma (duty) is known only through Vedic injunction (codanā, or vidhi):cite[sep]:cite[iep]:cite[wiki]. Daniel Arnold explains why: dharma is bhaviṣyat, “yet to be”, and perception reaches only what is present:cite[sep]. The model is an injunction telling one who desires heaven to sacrifice:cite[sep].:note[Jaimini’s sūtras say nothing of liberation (mokṣa); in Ranganathan’s account Kumārila is the first of the school to make room for it, alongside dharma:cite[iep].]

Jaimini's fifth sūtra calls the tie between word and meaning autpattika, “primordial”:cite[sep]; Ranganathan's rendering is “inborn”:cite[iep]. For the school the Veda is therefore apauruṣeya, authorless and eternal, and no author's error can falsify its injunctions:cite[sep]:cite[sep-epistemology]. Stephen Phillips calls Mīmāṃsā atheistic, though not everyone accepts the label:cite[sep-epistemology]:cite[wiki-prabhakara].:note[Arnold likens Kumārila’s critique of theism to the Buddhists’:cite[sep]. The school has also been described as holding both theistic and non-theistic doctrines:cite[wiki], and Prābhākara texts are reported to reject only the inference to God, not God:cite[wiki-prabhakara]. For Āpadeva an all-knowing God recalls the Veda from earlier world-ages and makes it known again in each, without composing it:cite[edgerton]. Almost all Nyāya thinkers, by contrast, make God (īśvara) the Veda’s author:cite[sep-epistemology].]

Building on Śabara, Kumārila argues for svataḥ prāmāṇya, which Arnold renders “immediate justification”:cite[sep]. On the reading Arnold prefers, every cognition is justified of itself until a later one overturns it:cite[sep].:note[To demand that another cognition vouch for each, he argues, would start an endless regress:cite[sep]. His commentators read the doctrine in two ways:cite[sep]. Uṃveka granted intrinsic validity only to cognitions that are in fact true; Pārthasārathimiśra, whose reading Arnold finds arguably the better, granted it to every cognition that presents itself as true, until something overturns it:cite[sep].] Both branches, Phillips writes, hold knowledge to be self-certifying, by different routes:cite[sep-epistemology].:note[On the Prābhākara account nothing beyond a true cognition’s own origin is needed to know that it is true:cite[sep-epistemology]. Bhāṭṭas infer its truth from the “cognizedness” it leaves on its object, and need an outside check only to learn that a cognition is false:cite[sep-epistemology].] Nyāya takes the opposite, extrinsic view, parataḥ prāmāṇya: to know that one knows takes a second cognition, an apperception (anuvyavasāya):cite[sep-epistemology].

Prābhākaras accept five means of knowledge (pramāṇa): perception, inference, testimony, comparison and postulation (arthāpatti):cite[wiki-pramana].:note[Postulation assumes the one thing that would explain a fact: a man who stays fat but never eats by day must eat at night. Mīmāṃsā defends it as a means of knowing in its own right, while Nyāya counts it a kind of inference:cite[sep-epistemology].] Bhāṭṭas add a sixth, non-perception (anupalabdhi), through which absences are known:cite[wiki-pramana]:cite[sep-epistemology]. The map shows the Bhāṭṭa six; [Pramāṇa-śāstra](/pramana-sastra) says which branch it follows.

On the school's view a rite's reward, such as heaven, may arrive only after the sacrificer has died:cite[sep]:cite[edgerton]. Apūrva spans the wait: what Edgerton calls a “transcendental power”, created by the rite and held until the reward falls due:cite[edgerton]. Kumārila argued for it by postulation; Arnold finds it hard not to see it as ad hoc:cite[sep].:note[Arnold adds that it looks less ad hoc once the duty the injunctions impose is taken as given:cite[sep]. Kumārila placed apūrva in the self of the one who sacrifices; Prabhākara placed it in the act:cite[wiki-apurva].]

Āpadeva's 17th-century primer sorts the Veda into injunctions (vidhi), formulas (mantra), names of rites (nāmadheya), prohibitions (niṣedha) and explanatory passages (arthavāda):cite[edgerton]:cite[wiki]. In it injunctions are the heart, a prohibition is an injunction to refrain, and the other three count only as aids to one or the other:cite[edgerton]. Edgerton calls Mīmāṃsā a “legal logic” reading the Veda as ritual law, and Hindu law (Dharmaśāstra) borrowed its rules of interpretation:cite[edgerton]:cite[wiki].:note[Kumārila accepted the remembered tradition (smṛti) as authoritative only where it rests on the Veda, if need be on a Vedic text since lost:cite[sep].]
