In Vedic myth, māyā is magical power, wielded by gods and their enemies alike:cite[wiki]. The Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad (4.9–10) turns the image on the cosmos: nature is māyā, and the great Lord its magician:cite[wiki].

For [Advaita Vedānta](/advaita-vedanta), māyā is the power by which the one [brahman](/brahman) appears as a world of many things without itself changing:cite[iep]. In each person it works as ignorance (avidyā):cite[iep], which takes the empirical ego for the self:cite[brit]. In the stock illustration a rope is mistaken for a snake:cite[iep]. Seeing a snake changes nothing in the rope, and the snake has no being apart from it:cite[sep]; once the rope is seen the snake is gone, and knowledge of brahman cancels, or “sublates”, the world in the same way:cite[iep]. Such appearances are, in Advaita's term, anirvacanīya:cite[iep].:note[Anirvacanīya means “indeterminable”:cite[sep], neither real nor unreal:cite[iep]. The snake is not real, since seeing the rope removes it, yet not unreal, since it really is seen, unlike a sky-lotus or a barren woman’s son, which are impossible:cite[iep]. Neil Dalal reads Śaṅkara as giving the whole world of names and forms this status, indeterminable as brahman or as other than brahman:cite[sep]; the later tradition, Sengaku Mayeda notes, called avidyā “indefinable as real or unreal”:cite[wiki-advaita]. Advaita texts also give passing illusions like the snake an order of their own, the prātibhāsika:cite[iep]. Dalal also reads Śaṅkara as denying that brahman ever really transforms into the world:cite[sep].] Advaita thus distinguishes a conventional order of reality (vyāvahārika), in which the world and its persons are real for all ordinary purposes, from the ultimate (pāramārthika), in which only brahman is:cite[sep].

How much of this is Śaṅkara's own is disputed:cite[sep]. He seldom uses the word:cite[sep], and Paul Hacker found it carries “hardly any terminological weight” for him:cite[wiki-advaita]. Hacker and Hajime Nakamura noted that Śaṅkara did not teach vivarta, the world as mere appearance:cite[wiki-advaita]; Andrew Nicholson thinks it took shape gradually after him:cite[iep-bhedabheda], and Sengaku Mayeda credits its definition to Prakāśātman of the Vivaraṇa school:cite[wiki-advaita].

For Rāmānuja the world is real and our knowledge of it genuine, since it displays God's glory (vibhūti):cite[brit-ramanuja]. His objections to avidyā and māyā are counted as seven, the subject of a study by John Grimes:cite[wiki-ramanuja]. Ignorance, he argues, needs someone to be ignorant: not brahman, whose nature is knowledge, and not an individual self, since individuals are what it was meant to explain:cite[iep-ramanuja].

Other texts use the word differently: in the [Bhagavad Gītā](/bhagavad-gita) (7.25) Kṛṣṇa's yogamāyā veils him from the world; Dvaita makes māyā God's own power over a real world; Jain ethics counts it, as deceit, among four passions; Buddhists liken consciousness, and Mahāyāna texts all phenomena, to a magic show, and Mahāyāna Abhidharma lists māyā as pretension, a mental factor:cite[wiki].
